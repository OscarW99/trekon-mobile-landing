# The Norwegian Method: Double Threshold Training Explained

If you follow distance running at all, you have likely heard about how Jakob Ingebrigtsen trains. At the center of his rise to Olympic gold and multiple world records is a deceptively simple idea.

**The Norwegian Method** in its purest form means performing two controlled, sub-threshold interval sessions in a single day, morning and evening, rather than one hard workout.

![Norwegian runner completing a morning threshold interval session on a track](/blog/assets/the-norwegian-method-double-threshold-training-explained/image_1.jpg)

The approach keeps both sessions within a blood lactate range of roughly 2 to 3 mmol/L, well below the point of serious fatigue.

This allows runners to accumulate far more quality threshold work per week than a traditional single hard session would permit, without the deep recovery cost that maximal-intensity training demands.

Pioneered by Marius Bakken and later applied systematically by coach Gjert Ingebrigtsen, the method has since spread well beyond Norway. Triathletes, marathoners, and everyday runners are now adapting its principles.

## What Is the Norwegian Method?

The Norwegian Method grew out of Scandinavia's distance running culture in the 1990s and 2000s. Marius Bakken, a two-time Olympian, refined the approach through thousands of lactate tests over many years.

Coach Gjert Ingebrigtsen applied Bakken's framework to his sons, Jakob, Filip, and Henrik. Jakob's Olympic gold in Tokyo and subsequent world records put the method on every coach's radar.

The philosophy rests on three core principles:

- Sub-threshold intensity: keep blood lactate below the maximal lactate steady state
- High weekly volume: accumulate far more quality work than hard-only programs allow
- Low injury risk: controlled effort reduces overtraining and breakdown

## The Norwegian Method: Double Threshold Training Explained

At its core, double threshold training splits quality work across two daily sessions - morning and evening - rather than concentrating effort into one exhausting bout. Sessions are typically 6-8 hours apart.

The key is the intensity ceiling. Both sessions stay within a blood lactate range of 2.0-3.0 mmol/L, comfortably below LT2, the maximal lactate steady state. This differs from classical tempo running, which often pushes runners above that ceiling.

### What "Double Threshold" Actually Means

LT2, or the maximal lactate steady state, is the highest intensity at which the body can still clear lactate as fast as it produces it. Double threshold work targets the zone just below this point.

Training below MLSS avoids glycolytic damage. A heart rate of roughly 80-87% of maximum is a practical proxy when a lactate meter is unavailable.

### Why Two Sessions Beat One

![Runner checking a blood lactate meter at trackside during a double threshold workout](/blog/assets/the-norwegian-method-double-threshold-training-explained/image_2.jpg)

Splitting threshold work into two shorter bouts triggers repeated molecular signaling. Each lactate spike activates MCT1/MCT4 transporters and PGC-1α pathways, driving mitochondrial biogenesis more effectively than a single longer session.

Weekly threshold accumulation can reach 100-180 minutes across multiple double days. A single hard tempo session rarely produces more than 30-40 minutes of genuine threshold work before fatigue forces a slowdown.

## The Physiology Behind Double Threshold Training

Lactate clearance limits performance in distance running. The Norwegian method trains the body's ability to transport and clear lactate through repeated, controlled exposure.

Mitochondrial density and MCT transporter capacity both increase with this training. More mitochondria mean faster aerobic energy production; better transporters mean lactate clears before it accumulates and forces a slowdown.

The method sits at the high-volume end of the polarised training model, where roughly 80% of weekly running stays in easy Zones 1-2 and the remaining 20% targets threshold.

### How Lactate Monitoring Controls Intensity

A portable lactate meter gives real-time feedback after each interval. The adjustment rule is simple:

- If lactate rises above 3.0 mmol/L, reduce pace on the next interval
- If lactate reads below 2.0 mmol/L, increase pace slightly
- If lactate sits between 2.0 and 3.0 mmol/L, hold current pace

Runners without a meter can use feel: at sub-threshold intensity, you should be able to speak roughly five words without gasping.

## How to Structure a Double Threshold Training Week

A typical Norwegian-method week includes 2-3 double threshold days, separated by at least 2-3 recovery days. Easy runs fill the remaining days.

Morning sessions use shorter intervals at LT1 pace, such as 4-6 x 6 minutes or 5 x 1000 m. Evening sessions step up to longer repeats, for example 8-10 x 1000 m or 6 x 2000 m, still below LT2.

### A Sample Double Threshold Training Week

| Day | Session | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | Easy 60-90 min, Zone 2 | Full recovery |
| Tuesday | Double threshold day | Morning + evening intervals |
| Wednesday | Easy recovery run | Low stress |
| Thursday | Optional second double day or single threshold | Mileage dependent |
| Friday | Easy run | Active recovery |
| Saturday | Long run or X-session | Race-specific effort |
| Sunday | Rest or easy jog | Complete recovery |

### Scaling the Method for Recreational Runners

European 10K record holder Andreas Almgren recommends a minimum of roughly 75 km per week before adopting double threshold days. Below that baseline, VO2max sessions may deliver greater gains.

Recreational runners can scale toward the method gradually:

- Start with 1-2 threshold sessions per week on separate days
- Build consistency before adding a second session on the same day
- Prioritize easy-day discipline to avoid compounding fatigue

## Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

![Runner pacing themselves on an empty road at dawn, representing controlled sub-threshold effort and disciplined training execution](/blog/assets/the-norwegian-method-double-threshold-training-explained/image_3.jpg)

The biggest mistake is treating double threshold days as two hard tempo runs. Pushing both sessions above 3.0 mmol/L turns a repeatable aerobic stimulus into an overtraining spiral.

Running too many double days before building sufficient mileage is the second common error. Without a strong aerobic base, accumulated fatigue builds across the week.

Watch for these warning signs that intensity or frequency has crept too high:

- Resting heart rate elevated 5 or more beats above normal on waking
- Lactate readings that keep rising across a session rather than stabilizing
- Performance declining across consecutive threshold days rather than holding steady

Skipping easy days to add mileage is equally damaging. The easy runs carry the aerobic volume that makes threshold sessions productive.

## Who Should Try Double Threshold Training?

The method suits a specific type of runner. Attempting it without the right foundation leads to burnout.

Good candidates include:

- Runners covering 75 km or more per week with a consistent aerobic base
- Competitive distance runners from 5K through the marathon
- Triathletes using the Blummenfelt model across disciplines
- Athletes who have plateaued on single-session threshold work

The method is not a good fit for:

- Beginners or runners training fewer than four days per week
- Anyone currently under-recovered or managing a soft-tissue injury
- Runners who cannot commit to keeping easy days genuinely easy

## Key Takeaways

The Norwegian Method comes down to one discipline: accumulating large amounts of controlled sub-threshold work by splitting it across two daily sessions, kept within a blood lactate range of 2.0-3.0 mmol/L.

Weekly threshold totals of 100-180 minutes are achievable through this method, far beyond what a single hard session can safely produce. The adaptation driver is frequency and consistency, not any single brutal workout.

Start conservatively, build frequency only after your base is solid, and treat intensity control as the non-negotiable foundation the whole system rests on.
