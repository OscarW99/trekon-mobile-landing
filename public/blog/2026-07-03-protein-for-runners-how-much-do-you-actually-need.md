# Protein for Runners: How Much Do You Actually Need?

Ask most runners about nutrition, and carbohydrates dominate the conversation. But protein deserves just as much attention. With every footstrike, your body absorbs forces up to seven times your bodyweight, and protein is what helps your muscles rebuild and recover from that repeated stress.

![Runner eating a protein-rich meal after a training run](/blog/assets/protein-for-runners-how-much-do-you-actually-need/image_1.jpg)

The standard dietary guideline for adults recommends just 0.8 grams of protein per kilogram of bodyweight per day, designed to prevent deficiency in sedentary people. For runners, that number falls well short. Sports nutrition research consistently points to a much higher range, roughly 1.4 to 2.0 grams per kilogram per day, depending on training volume, intensity, and individual goals.

## Why Protein Matters More Than Most Runners Think

Running is a repetitive, high-impact sport. Your muscle fibers experience constant micro-damage that only protein can repair. Beyond muscle, protein supports your immune system, strengthens bones, and helps form hemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen to working muscles.

The carbs-only mindset is one of the most persistent myths in running nutrition. Carbohydrates fuel your runs, but protein rebuilds you afterward. Without enough of it, you risk slower recovery, higher injury rates, and performance plateaus that no amount of extra mileage will fix.

## How Much Protein Do Runners Actually Need?

Sports nutrition guidelines from the International Society of Sports Nutrition support a range of 1.4 to 2.0 g/kg/day for physically active individuals. A 2025 narrative review published in *Sports Medicine* found that while endurance athletes typically consume around 1.5 g/kg/day, the evidence supports targeting approximately [1.8 g/kg/day to optimize recovery and training adaptation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12152099/). During carbohydrate-restricted phases or rest days, needs may climb above 2.0 g/kg/day.

Practical examples:

| Runner | Bodyweight | Daily Protein Target (1.4-1.8 g/kg) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Moderate runner | 68 kg (150 lb) | 95-122 g/day |
| Serious runner | 70 kg | 98-126 g/day |
| High-volume runner | 80 kg | 112-144 g/day |

### How Training Load Changes Your Needs

Your protein requirements shift based on what your training looks like week to week.

- **Casual runners** can sit at the lower end, around 1.4 g/kg/day.
- **High-volume training blocks** push needs toward 1.8 g/kg/day or higher.
- **Rest days and carb-restricted days** actually require more protein, not less.
- **Injury or recovery phases** elevate requirements further, since tissue healing demands additional amino acids.
- **Runners aged 55 and over** benefit from the upper end of the range to counteract age-related muscle loss.

### Calculate Your Own Target

Take your bodyweight in kilograms and multiply by a factor between 1.4 and 1.8, depending on training intensity.

Example for a 68 kg runner in a moderate training phase:

- Lower end: 68 x 1.4 = 95 g/day
- Moderate target: 68 x 1.6 = 109 g/day
- Higher end: 68 x 1.8 = 122 g/day

Adjust upward during peak training blocks, injury recovery, or low-carbohydrate phases.

## When to Eat Protein: Timing Strategies for Runners

Hitting your daily target matters more than precise timing, but distribution still makes a meaningful difference. Spreading protein evenly across the day, roughly every 3 to 4 hours, keeps amino acids available for continuous muscle repair.

Post-run timing is worth prioritising. Consuming protein within 30 to 45 minutes after finishing a run supports repair while your body is most receptive. A practical post-run target is 0.25 to 0.30 g per kilogram of bodyweight, roughly 17 to 20 grams for a 150-pound runner. Liquid forms like protein shakes digest faster, which is useful when appetite is low after a hard effort.

One important caveat: your body can only use around 15 to 30 grams of protein effectively per meal for muscle protein synthesis. Eating 80 grams in one sitting does not produce twice the benefit of eating 40 grams.

## The Best Protein Sources for Runners

Both animal and plant sources can meet a runner's protein needs. The key difference is completeness: animal proteins contain all nine essential amino acids, while most plant proteins are low in one or more.

**Animal sources** (complete proteins):
- Eggs, chicken breast, turkey, lean beef
- Salmon, tuna, and other fish
- Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, milk

**Plant sources** (often incomplete individually):
- Soy products such as tofu, tempeh, and edamame (complete)
- Lentils, chickpeas, black beans
- Quinoa (complete), hemp seeds, pumpkin seeds

Plant-based runners can meet their needs by combining sources across the day, for example pairing rice with beans or adding seeds to legume-based meals. Protein supplements such as whey, casein, or plant-based powders are a practical backup when food intake falls short, particularly in the post-run window when appetite is low.

## Signs You're Not Getting Enough Protein

![Variety of high-protein foods including meat, fish, eggs, legumes, dairy, and nuts laid out together](/blog/assets/protein-for-runners-how-much-do-you-actually-need/image_2.jpg)

Your body gives clear signals when protein intake is insufficient:

- Persistent muscle soreness that lingers well beyond 48 hours
- Frequent injuries, including stress fractures, indicating poor tissue repair
- Energy lulls throughout the day despite eating enough total calories
- Stalled performance despite consistent training

## Common Protein Myths Runners Should Stop Believing

Several myths keep runners from eating enough protein. Here is what the evidence actually shows:

- **Myth: High protein harms kidneys.** For healthy athletes with normal kidney function, there is no evidence that intakes in the 1.4 to 2.0 g/kg range cause harm.
- **Myth: Protein weakens bones.** This is not supported by current research. Adequate protein is associated with better bone density, not worse.
- **Myth: Only strength athletes need protein.** Endurance training breaks down muscle tissue just as significantly. Runners have the same repair needs.
- **Myth: More protein at one meal means more muscle.** Per-meal limits apply. Excess protein beyond roughly 30 grams per sitting does not accelerate synthesis further.

Carbohydrates remain essential. Protein does not replace carbohydrate fueling, and neglecting carbs to chase higher protein targets will hurt performance.

## Key Takeaways

![Infographic showing protein intake ranges in grams per kilogram for casual versus serious runners](/blog/assets/protein-for-runners-how-much-do-you-actually-need/image_3.jpg)

- Target 1.4 to 2.0 g/kg of bodyweight per day, with most runners benefiting from aiming around 1.8 g/kg
- Distribute protein evenly across meals, roughly every 3 to 4 hours
- Prioritise a post-run dose of 17 to 20 g within 45 minutes of finishing
- Adjust upward during high training load, low-carbohydrate phases, injury recovery, or if you are over 55
- Use a food-first approach and treat supplements as a convenient backup, not a foundation
- Personalise your target based on bodyweight, current training phase, and individual goals, then reassess as your training evolves
