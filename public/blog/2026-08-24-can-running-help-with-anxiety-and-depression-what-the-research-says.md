# Can Running Help with Anxiety and Depression? What the Research Says

**Can running help with anxiety and depression?** For millions of people living with low mood, persistent worry, or clinical depression, the idea that lacing up a pair of shoes could meaningfully shift their mental state sounds almost too simple. Yet a growing body of clinical studies, systematic reviews, and brain imaging research suggests the connection is very real.

![Person running outdoors on a trail, looking calm and focused, representing the mental health benefits of running](/blog/assets/can-running-help-with-anxiety-and-depression-what-the-research-says/image_1.jpg)

One landmark study found that 16 weeks of running therapy produced remission rates nearly identical to those of antidepressant medication, while also delivering measurable improvements in cardiovascular and metabolic health. Whether you are managing a diagnosed condition or simply trying to feel better day to day, the evidence below breaks down what running can and cannot do for anxiety and depression.

## What the Science Actually Shows About Running and Mental Health

The research consensus is clear: running and other forms of aerobic exercise are consistently associated with meaningful improvements in mood, anxiety, and depressive symptoms across a wide range of populations. A large overview of systematic reviews published in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* found that physical activity produces medium beneficial effects on depression (median effect size approximately -0.43) and anxiety (approximately -0.42) compared with usual care. These benefits appear in clinical and non-clinical groups alike, from healthy adults to people managing chronic conditions.

Key findings include:

- Mood improvements are reported across varying running durations and intensities
- Benefits extend to self-esteem, emotional stability, and overall psychological wellbeing
- Both biological and psychological mechanisms appear to drive the effects

## Key Studies on Running as a Therapeutic Tool

Several well-designed studies have examined running specifically as a therapeutic intervention.

The MOTAR study is perhaps the most cited. Over 16 weeks, 141 adults with depression and anxiety disorders were assigned to either antidepressant medication or group-based running therapy. Remission rates were 44.8% for antidepressants and 43.3% for running therapy, a difference that was not statistically significant. What did differ was physical health: running therapy led to measurable improvements in weight, waist circumference, blood pressure, and heart rate variability, while antidepressants were associated with less favourable changes in these same markers.

A secondary analysis focused on immunometabolic depression found that running therapy produced greater improvements in the metabolic syndrome index (d = 0.59) compared with antidepressants, which showed a tendency to worsen related measures. For patients with prominent inflammatory or metabolic features, the [antidepressants or running therapy study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36828150/) suggests running may offer meaningful added benefits.

A separate 12-week programme enrolled 46 participants with complex mood disorders and recorded significant reductions in depression (p < 0.0001), anxiety (p < 0.0001), and perceived stress (p = 0.01). Higher attendance was directly linked to greater symptom improvement.

### Intensity and Frequency

Not all running produces identical results:

- **Low intensity** (40-49% HRmax): benefits emerge more gradually over 12 weeks
- **Moderate intensity** (50-59% HRmax): steady, ongoing improvement throughout
- **High intensity** (60-69% HRmax): earlier gains, with notable improvements at the midpoint

Research suggests that running once or twice per week can match the mental health impact of more frequent sessions. Consistency over time appears more important than volume.

## How Running Changes the Brain

Running physically reshapes the brain through several mechanisms:

- **Hippocampal growth**: the hippocampus increases in volume with regular aerobic exercise, supporting memory and mood regulation
- **Neurotransmitter modulation**: running elevates serotonin, dopamine, and endorphins, all linked to mood stabilisation
- **Endocannabinoids**: released during sustained running, these contribute to reduced anxiety and the sensation sometimes called runner's high
- **Cortisol reduction**: regular running lowers baseline levels of the primary stress hormone

A seven-week moderate-intensity running intervention in 21 healthy young men produced measurable increases in hippocampal volume alongside significant falls in depression scores. A separate fMRI study found that 16 weeks of running therapy reduced activity in the Default Mode Network (associated with rumination) while increasing activity in visual-somatomotor-subcortical networks. Depressive symptoms fell significantly, suggesting running reorganises the neural architecture underlying mood disorders rather than simply masking symptoms.

## The Role of Group Running and Social Support

The format in which you run matters. Across multiple studies, group-based and supervised running consistently outperforms solo exercise for depression outcomes. The [12-week group running programme research](https://bmjopensem.bmj.com/content/4/1/e000314/) found that perceived friendship within the running group and attendance rates both correlated directly with mood improvements. Participants who felt a stronger sense of belonging showed the greatest reductions in perceived stress.

Professional guidance and structured settings add further benefit by improving self-efficacy and providing accountability, two psychological factors that independently support recovery from depression and anxiety.

## Who Benefits Most and What the Research Still Needs

Research points to several groups who show particularly strong responses:

- **Emerging adults (18-30)**: the largest effect sizes for depression reduction appear in this age group
- **Postnatal and perinatal females**: physical activity interventions show strong benefits in this population
- **Adolescents**: the 12-15 age group responds well to structured running programmes, particularly for anxiety reduction
- **People with chronic conditions**: those managing metabolic or inflammatory disorders show meaningful improvements

Key limitations remain. Many studies use participant preference designs rather than full randomisation, demographic diversity in samples is limited, and high-quality controlled trials focused specifically on running are still needed. A small number of studies also flag adverse outcomes, including exercise addiction in some individuals.

## How to Start Running for Mental Health

![Runner stretching before a morning run in a park, preparing for a structured training session](/blog/assets/can-running-help-with-anxiety-and-depression-what-the-research-says/image_2.jpg)

If the research has convinced you to try running as a mental health tool, starting well matters more than starting fast:

- Begin with short, moderate-intensity runs of 10 to 20 minutes; mood benefits can emerge after just 10 minutes
- Prioritise consistency over distance; once or twice weekly is enough to see meaningful effects
- Follow a structured 12-week progressive plan to build gradually and reduce injury risk
- Consider joining a group run or running club to access the social benefits the evidence highlights
- Use running as a complement to, not a replacement for, any existing treatment plan

If you have an existing health condition or are managing a diagnosed mental health disorder, speak with a clinician before beginning a new exercise programme. Running works best as one component of a broader strategy that may include therapy, medication, or other evidence-based approaches.

## Key Takeaways: Running as a Tool for Mental Wellbeing

The evidence supporting running for anxiety and depression is substantial and growing. Here is what the research consistently shows:

- Running reduces anxiety and depressive symptoms across clinical and non-clinical populations
- Biological mechanisms include hippocampal growth, neurotransmitter modulation, endocannabinoid release, and cortisol reduction
- Group and supervised settings amplify the benefits, particularly for depression
- Consistency matters more than intensity or frequency; moderate effort, done regularly, delivers results
- Running is most effective as part of a holistic mental health strategy, not as a single solution
