# Ground Contact Time and Vertical Oscillation: Do They Matter

You finish a run, glance at your watch, and see a string of numbers: ground contact time 245 ms, vertical oscillation 9.2 cm. Your app color-codes them, implying you should do something about it. But understanding **Ground Contact Time and Vertical Oscillation: Do They Matter** requires looking past the marketing and into the actual science.

![Runner's foot striking the ground showing ground contact time and vertical oscillation biomechanics](/blog/assets/ground-contact-time-and-vertical-oscillation-do-they-matter/image_1.jpg)

Both metrics are real biomechanical variables. Ground contact time measures how long your foot stays on the ground each step, typically 200-300 ms for recreational runners. Vertical oscillation measures how much your torso bounces vertically, usually 6-13 cm.

Research confirms they correlate with running economy, but the relationship is more complicated than most apps suggest.

The key insight from sports science: these numbers are outcomes, not levers. They reflect your speed, fitness, and mechanics rather than causes you can directly manipulate. Track your own trends across identical sessions - improving numbers signal genuine fitness gains.

## What Are Ground Contact Time and Vertical Oscillation?

Ground contact time (GCT) is the duration in milliseconds your foot stays in contact with the ground during each running step. Vertical oscillation (VO) measures how far your torso moves vertically with each stride, expressed in centimeters.

Modern watches capture both metrics via accelerometer - built into the watch, inside a chest strap such as Garmin's HRM-Pro, or in a waist-mounted running pod.

Typical ranges differ significantly by running level:

- Recreational runners: GCT roughly 200-300 ms, VO roughly 6-13 cm
- Elite runners: GCT roughly 160-210 ms, VO closer to 6-8 cm
- Sensor validity: cadence is highest (ICC 0.931), VO is reliable (ICC 0.963), while GCT has moderate agreement with motion-capture systems (ICC 0.749)

## Why Ground Contact Time and Vertical Oscillation: Do They Matter for Running Economy

![Infographic illustrating how ground reaction force, leg stiffness, and vertical oscillation connect to running economy and metabolic oxygen cost](/blog/assets/ground-contact-time-and-vertical-oscillation-do-they-matter/image_2.jpg)

Running economy is the oxygen cost of running at a given pace - the lower, the more efficient the runner. Both GCT and VO influence this metric through the mechanics of force production and elastic energy return.

The propulsion phase shows the strongest direct link to running economy, according to [a systematic review of modifiable biomechanical factors](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4887549/). Shorter GCT reflects greater leg stiffness, allowing elastic energy to be stored and returned faster.

Lower VO reduces oxygen cost by limiting the metabolic work of lifting your center of mass. The effect is real but modest, and results vary by sex, shoe mass, and individual mechanics.

## The Problem With Chasing These Numbers

GCT and VO are strongly pace-dependent. As speed increases, GCT naturally shortens and VO adjusts accordingly, so a 230 ms reading at 5:30/km pace says almost nothing about form quality compared to a 190 ms reading at 4:00/km.

Forcing a consciously shorter GCT tends to backfire. The nervous system self-tunes contact time to minimise metabolic cost, and overriding it typically disrupts economy rather than improving it.

Pitfalls to avoid when reading these numbers:

- Comparing your GCT or VO to another runner without matching pace, terrain, and body proportions
- Reducing VO so aggressively that stride length shrinks and overall speed drops
- Treating app colour-coded bands as strict personal targets rather than population medians
- Drawing conclusions from a single run rather than a consistent trend across comparable sessions

No universal optimal value exists. Individual body structure, speed, and training history mean one runner's efficient 260 ms GCT can outperform another's forced 210 ms.

## When These Metrics Actually Tell You Something Useful

### Tracking Changes Across Matched Sessions

The most valid use case is self-comparison across identical sessions - same route, pace, shoes, and conditions - where changes reflect genuine fitness adaptation.

A gradual GCT decrease over four to eight weeks, while pace stays constant and heart rate holds steady or drops, is a reliable fitness signal.

Healthy progression signs across matched repeat sessions:

- GCT trending downward by a few milliseconds over several weeks at the same pace
- Heart rate falling while effort and pace remain unchanged
- Cadence rising slightly without deliberate manipulation

A rising GCT under identical load can indicate accumulating fatigue, shoe midsole breakdown, or an early compensation pattern worth investigating.

### Reading Vertical Oscillation as a Fatigue Marker

VO is especially informative during long runs. A spike in VO during the final kilometres, compared to identical pace in the opening kilometres, signals that elastic energy return is degrading under accumulated fatigue.

Asymmetry in ground contact balance - where one foot consistently stays on the ground longer than the other - can flag injury compensation before pain becomes obvious. Catching this trend early provides useful lead time.

## The Real Lever: Cadence

Cadence (steps per minute) is the most accessible and directly controllable input in running mechanics. A modest 5% increase, for example from 158 to 165 steps per minute, reduces both GCT and VO as downstream effects without any need for conscious stride manipulation.

The same cadence increase also reduces loading at the knee, which is a clinically meaningful benefit beyond efficiency.

Apply cadence changes gradually:

- Establish your natural cadence on a comfortable easy run before targeting any change
- Increase by 2-3% over 2-3 weeks before nudging further
- Use a metronome cue or rhythmic audio prompt to anchor the new rate without forcing it

## How to Improve GCT and VO the Right Way

The underlying drivers of better GCT and VO are leg stiffness, rapid force production, and ankle and glute mechanics - developed through training load, not conscious stride manipulation.

![Runner performing single-leg plyometric box jumps in a gym, building the rapid force production that improves ground contact time and running mechanics](/blog/assets/ground-contact-time-and-vertical-oscillation-do-they-matter/image_3.jpg)

Strength and plyometric work build the elastic energy return system that naturally shortens contact time. Single-leg jumps, calf raises, and glute exercises are practical starting points. Faster running itself also develops leg spring stiffness.

## How to Use These Metrics in a Running App

[Trekon](https://www.trekon.run) tracks GCT and VO alongside heart rate, pace, and training load across sessions, making multi-week trends visible rather than leaving them buried in individual run data.

The most effective way to use these metrics within a structured plan:

- Compare GCT and VO only within the same effort zone - easy runs against easy runs, threshold against threshold
- Flag sessions where GCT rises while pace and heart rate hold steady as potential fatigue or shoe-wear signals
- Use VO spikes in the final third of a long run as one input into weekly load decisions alongside perceived effort and recovery quality

Avoid drawing conclusions from a single session. A direction across five or six comparable sessions is meaningful; one outlier almost never is.

## Key Takeaways: Ground Contact Time and Vertical Oscillation: Do They Matter

GCT and VO are legitimate biomechanical indicators backed by research. They matter as signals of underlying fitness and mechanics, not as targets to manipulate directly.

The practical summary:

- Train the underlying causes - leg stiffness, rapid force production, cadence - and GCT and VO will improve as natural results
- Compare your numbers to your own previous numbers at matched pace and conditions, using [reference values for recreational runners](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10588426/) as rough clinical benchmarks rather than rigid goals
- Use cadence as the primary lever; GCT and VO follow from it
- Read late-run VO spikes and rising GCT as fatigue signals worth investigating, not form failures requiring immediate overhaul
- Individual context always outweighs population averages when interpreting any single metric reading
