# How to Write Your Own Training Plan If You Can't Afford a Coach

Knowing **how to write your own training plan if you can't afford a coach** is one of the most practical fitness skills you can develop. Personal coaching delivers real value, but it comes at a price many people cannot justify. The good news is that the core principles behind effective program design are not secret knowledge, and applying them yourself is entirely achievable.

![Runner reviewing a handwritten training plan on a notepad beside running shoes and a water bottle](/blog/assets/how-to-write-your-own-training-plan-if-you-cant-afford-a-coach/image_1.png)

Most self-made plans fall apart not because the person lacks discipline, but because the plan itself was never built to last. The fix is structure, not willpower. This guide walks you through the same foundational decisions a coach would make: setting a clear goal, choosing a realistic weekly schedule, selecting the right movements, and building in progression and recovery.

## Step 1: Define a Specific, Measurable Goal

Every effective training plan starts with a single, concrete goal. Vague intentions like "get fitter" give your plan nothing to anchor to.

Write your goal as a sentence with a number, a deadline, and a result:

- "Run a sub-25 minute 5K by September 1"
- "Deadlift 120 kg for 3 reps by the end of December"
- "Complete a half-marathon in under 2 hours by spring"

If you cannot write that sentence, your goal is not specific enough yet. The goal determines your rep ranges, weekly structure, exercise selection, and how you measure success.

## Step 2: Assess Your Current Fitness Level Honestly

Before writing a single session, take stock of where you actually are. Overestimating your starting point is one of the most common reasons self-written plans collapse in week three.

Ask yourself: How many days per week can I realistically train? Do I have gym access or am I training at home? What is my current fitness benchmark, and where are my obvious weak points or injury history? Honest answers here shape every decision that follows.

## Step 3: Choose the Right Weekly Structure

Your weekly structure determines how you distribute sessions and how often each muscle group gets trained. The right split is the one you can sustain.

| Days Available | Recommended Split |
| --- | --- |
| 2 days | Full-body A/B |
| 3 days | Full-body A/B/C |
| 4 days | Upper-lower |
| 5-6 days | Push-pull-legs |

Training each muscle group at least twice per week produces better results than once-weekly approaches. For most people, three to four sessions per week hits the sweet spot between stimulus and recovery.

For runners, around 70 to 80 percent of weekly mileage should be at an easy, conversational pace, with harder efforts making up the remainder.

## Step 4: Select Your Exercises and Movement Patterns

Fill each session with movements that cover the major patterns: squat, hinge, push, pull, and carry. Order exercises by skill and load demand, with high-skill movements like deadlifts going first when you are fresh.

A simple full-body session template:

1. Squat pattern - back squat, goblet squat, or split squat
2. Hinge pattern - deadlift, Romanian deadlift, or kettlebell swing
3. Push pattern - bench press, overhead press, or push-up variation
4. Pull pattern - pull-up, barbell row, or dumbbell row
5. Accessory - one or two targeted exercises for weak points

For beginners, prioritise movements you can perform with reasonable technique and that you actually enjoy.

## Step 5: Set Volume, Intensity, and Rep Ranges

Volume and intensity should match your primary goal:

| Goal | Rep Range | Sets per Exercise | Rest Period |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Strength | 3-6 reps | 3-5 | 2-4 minutes |
| Muscle gain | 6-12 reps | 3-4 | 60-90 seconds |
| Endurance | 12-20 reps | 2-3 | 30-60 seconds |

Aim for two to four hard sets per exercise per session. For runners, add no more than ten percent volume per week.

## Step 6: Build In Progressive Overload and Recovery

Progressive overload is the mechanism that forces your body to adapt. Over time, the training stimulus must increase. You can do this by adding weight, adding reps, adding a working set, or reducing rest periods slightly. Change only one variable at a time.

Recovery is equally non-negotiable. Every eight to twelve weeks, schedule a deload week where you reduce volume by roughly 40 to 50 percent while keeping intensity similar. Signs you need to back off sooner include persistent joint soreness, declining performance, and disrupted sleep. Plan recovery in advance rather than waiting until you feel broken.

## Step 7: Track Progress and Set Checkpoints

![Weekly training plan template spread across a desk with a pen and running shoes nearby](/blog/assets/how-to-write-your-own-training-plan-if-you-cant-afford-a-coach/image_2.jpg)

A plan without checkpoints is just a wish list. Review progress every four to six weeks and ask: Am I closer to my measurable goal? Has performance on key lifts or runs improved? Is recovery keeping pace with training load?

If progress stalls, adjust one variable and observe for two to three weeks before changing anything else. Overhauling the entire plan at the first sign of a plateau is one of the most common self-coaching mistakes. ## A Simple Sample Weekly Training Plan

**Beginner - 3-day full-body**

- Monday: Squat, press, row - 3 sets of 8-10 reps each
- Wednesday: Hinge, push variation, pull variation - 3 sets of 8-10 reps each
- Friday: Repeat Monday with a small load increase if all reps were completed cleanly

**Intermediate - 4-day upper-lower**

- Monday/Thursday: Upper body - press and row focus, 4 sets of 6-10 reps
- Tuesday/Friday: Lower body - squat and hinge focus, 4 sets of 6-10 reps

**Running - 4-day half-marathon week**

- Monday: Easy run, 40-50 minutes at conversational pace
- Wednesday: Tempo run, 20-25 minutes at comfortably hard effort
- Saturday: Long run, building toward 18-22 km over the training block

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

Even a well-intentioned plan can go wrong quickly. The most common errors are:

- **No clear goal** - the plan drifts and changes every two weeks based on mood
- **Too much volume too soon** - a reliable path to overuse injury
- **Skipping recovery** - treating rest days as wasted time rather than productive adaptation
- **No tracking** - without records you cannot confirm progress or spot problems early
- **No progression rules** - showing up consistently without a system for getting harder produces maintenance, not improvement

## Free Tools That Help You Self-Coach

Tracking your training does not require expensive software. A simple spreadsheet or notebook for logging sets, reps, and weights is often enough. Free apps can track running pace, distance, and heart rate. When evaluating any self-coaching tool, look for the ability to log workouts, visualise trends over time, and adjust the plan based on how you are actually responding.

## Frequently Asked Questions

![Person reviewing common training mistakes checklist on a clipboard at a gym](/blog/assets/how-to-write-your-own-training-plan-if-you-cant-afford-a-coach/image_3.jpg)

**Can beginners write their own training plan?**
Yes. Beginners benefit from simpler plans, and simple plans are easier to write. A three-day full-body structure with compound movements and basic progression is entirely manageable without coaching experience.

**How many days per week should I train?**
Three to four days per week is optimal for most people. It provides enough stimulus for meaningful adaptation while leaving room for recovery.

**How long should a self-written plan last?**
Work in four to six week blocks with a deload at the end of each. Plan three to four months ahead, then reassess your goal and structure at that point.

**When should I hire a coach?**
Consider it when progress has stalled for more than six to eight weeks despite consistent effort, or when you are dealing with recurring injury and need expert eyes on your movement.

**How do I know if my plan is working?**
Return to your measurable goal every four to six weeks. If your key metric - pace, weight on the bar, or weekly mileage - is moving in the right direction, the plan is working. If not, adjust one variable and give it three weeks before drawing conclusions.
