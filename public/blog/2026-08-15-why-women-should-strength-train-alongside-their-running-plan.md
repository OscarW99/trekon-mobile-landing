# Why Women Should Strength Train Alongside Their Running Plan

Understanding **why women should strength train alongside their running plan** is one of the most valuable things a female runner can do for her performance and long-term health. Many women log their miles and never touch a weight, yet research consistently shows that adding just one or two strength sessions per week can improve running economy, reduce injury risk, and support stronger muscles and bones across every age group.

![Woman performing a squat as part of a strength training routine to complement her running plan](/blog/assets/why-women-should-strength-train-alongside-their-running-plan/image_1.jpg)

Whether you are a casual jogger or following a structured race plan, strength training offers benefits that running alone simply cannot provide, from protecting connective tissues to preserving muscle quality as you age.

## The Case for Combining Strength and Running

One of the most persistent myths in women's fitness is that lifting weights causes bulking. In reality, meaningful muscle hypertrophy requires a sustained calorie surplus and training volumes far beyond what a typical running programme involves. Women also produce significantly less testosterone than men, making it physiologically difficult to build large muscle mass through standard strength work.

Concurrent training - combining strength and endurance in the same programme - is well-supported by research across all experience levels. Adding resistance work delivers benefits that running alone cannot replicate.

## The Science Behind Strength Training for Women Runners

The performance case for strength training rests on several interconnected mechanisms. Running economy - how efficiently your body uses oxygen at a given pace - improves when muscles and tendons become better at storing and releasing elastic energy. Stronger leg muscles also enhance neuromuscular coordination, meaning your nervous system recruits muscle fibres more effectively with each stride.

A [systematic review of 14 studies on concurrent training in healthy adult females](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37948036/) confirmed that combining both training modes improved strength and endurance performance, with additional benefits to body composition, and found no established interference effect in women.

### Running Economy and Injury Prevention

An 11-week study of well-trained female endurance athletes found that adding heavy resistance training twice weekly improved running economy without harming aerobic capacity. The mechanism centres on muscle-tendon stiffness: heavier loading increases stiffness of the leg extensor complex, improving elastic energy storage and return during each ground contact. Improvements in the range of 2-8% have been reported across studies.

Connective tissue resilience is equally important. Tendons, ligaments, and fascia adapt slowly to running load, and when they lag behind cardiovascular fitness, injury risk rises. Women also face a specific anatomical consideration: female athletes are two to eight times more likely to suffer ACL injuries than male counterparts, partly due to quad-hamstring strength imbalances. A 2018 BJSM meta-analysis found that adding strength training to an endurance routine reduced overuse injury risk by roughly 50%.

## Bone Health, Muscle Quality, and Healthy Ageing

![Active woman in her fifties performing a deadlift in a gym, illustrating strength training benefits for bone health and ageing](/blog/assets/why-women-should-strength-train-alongside-their-running-plan/image_2.jpg)

Sarcopenia - the gradual loss of muscle mass with age - begins earlier than most people expect and accelerates around perimenopause. Running helps, but it does not fully counteract this decline. Research found that active women who combined running with strength training had greater knee extensor strength and better muscle quality than inactive peers across both under-50 and over-50 age groups.

Bone mineral density is another critical consideration. Heavy compound lifts - squats, deadlifts, split squats - send a genuine loading signal to bone that running's repetitive low-force impact cannot match. A resistance training background is linked to higher bone density and fewer stress fractures, with the protective effect becoming particularly important through perimenopause and beyond.

### Hormonal Considerations for Women Runners

Menstrual cycle phase, hormonal contraceptive use, and menstrual dysfunction can all influence training responses and recovery. What is clear is that concurrent training is beneficial for women across hormonal contexts. Practically, this suggests personalising training phases around your cycle where possible and treating strength programming as a long-term investment rather than a short-term fix.

## What Exercises Should Women Runners Prioritise?

The most effective exercises for runners are compound lower-limb movements that mirror the demands of running. A well-structured programme should include:

- **Squats and goblet squats** - quad, glute, and knee stability
- **Deadlifts and Romanian deadlifts** - posterior chain strength and hamstring resilience
- **Split squats and lunges** - single-leg strength and hip stability
- **Calf raises** - ankle and Achilles tendon loading
- **Plyometrics** - combined strength-plyometric programmes show the strongest improvements in running economy

During high training load periods, isometric exercises such as wall sits and isometric calf holds provide a lower-fatigue alternative that still maintains tendon stiffness adaptations.

### How Many Sessions Per Week?

Two sessions per week is the minimum effective dose for meaningful adaptation. Each session can be as short as 20-40 minutes. [Women's Running's guide to strength training for runners](https://www.womensrunning.co.uk/training/the-ultimate-guide-to-strength-training-for-runners/) confirms this expert consensus, noting that even one or two sessions weekly produce noticeable improvements in power, efficiency, and injury resistance. Beginners benefit most from starting early rather than waiting for an injury to prompt action.

## How to Schedule Strength Training Alongside Your Running Plan

The interference effect - where fatigue from one session compromises the quality of another - is manageable with smart scheduling. Key rules:

- Avoid heavy strength sessions the day before a hard run or long run
- Pair a shorter run in the morning with a strength session in the afternoon if time is limited
- Place strength work on lighter running days or rest-adjacent days
- Allow at least one full day of recovery after heavy resistance work before a high-intensity run

Start at the beginning of a training cycle, not after an injury appears. An 8-12 week adaptation window means that runners who begin strength work early arrive at race day with stronger tendons, better neuromuscular coordination, and lower injury risk.

## Common Concerns Women Have About Strength Training

Most hesitation around strength training comes down to a handful of concerns, all of which have straightforward answers:

| Concern | Reality |
| --- | --- |
| "I'll gain weight or bulk up" | Hypertrophy requires a calorie surplus and high volume; standard runner-focused strength work does not produce this |
| "I don't have time" | Two sessions of 20-40 minutes per week is sufficient |
| "I don't have gym access" | Bodyweight split squats, lunges, single-leg calf raises, and plyometrics are highly effective |
| "I'll be too tired to run" | Smart scheduling eliminates most fatigue conflict |

## Key Takeaways: Why Every Woman Runner Should Add Strength Work

![Woman stretching after a combined strength and running workout session outdoors](/blog/assets/why-women-should-strength-train-alongside-their-running-plan/image_3.jpg)

The evidence is consistent and practical. Adding strength training to a running plan improves running economy by 2-8%, reduces overuse injury risk by roughly half, supports bone mineral density, and preserves muscle quality across decades of training.

- Two sessions per week is enough to see real results
- Compound lower-limb exercises deliver the greatest running-specific benefit
- Bone and muscle health gains are meaningful for women of all ages, with particular importance around perimenopause
- Start at the beginning of a training plan, not after an injury forces the issue
- Scheduling around hard run days prevents fatigue from undermining either quality

Strength training does not compete with running. For women, it is what makes running sustainable, efficient, and resilient for the long term.
