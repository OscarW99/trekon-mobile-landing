# Gut Training: How to Teach Your Stomach to Handle Race Fuel

You've trained your legs, built your aerobic base, and dialed in your pacing strategy. But there's one system most endurance athletes overlook until race day punishes them for it: the gut.

**Gut Training: How to Teach Your Stomach to Handle Race Fuel** is the practice of systematically conditioning your digestive system to absorb carbohydrates and fluids under the demands of hard exercise, so nausea, cramping, and bloating don't end your race before the finish line.

![Runner taking a gel during a long training run to practice gut training for race day](/blog/assets/gut-training-how-to-teach-your-stomach-to-handle-race-fuel/image_1.jpg)

When you exercise, blood flow shifts away from your digestive tract and toward working muscles, leaving your gut under-resourced and struggling to process fuel. Add heat, high intensity, and an unfamiliar product, and GI distress becomes almost inevitable for an untrained stomach.

The good news: the gut is highly adaptable. Research in Sports Medicine confirms that gastric emptying, stomach comfort, and carbohydrate absorption all improve with consistent, targeted practice. Just like your legs, your stomach responds to progressive training.

## Why Your Stomach Rebels During a Race

Several factors compound the problem during intense exercise:

- **Blood flow redirection**: at high intensity, gut blood supply can drop to roughly 20% of its resting level
- **Mechanical stress**: the repetitive bouncing of running physically disrupts GI absorption and motility
- **Pre-race anxiety**: stress hormones accelerate gut motility, increasing cramping and urgency risk
- **Dehydration**: even mild fluid loss reduces blood supply to the gut, slowing digestion and raising distress risk

## The Science Behind Gut Training: How to Teach Your Stomach to Handle Race Fuel

In a landmark review, sports scientist Asker Jeukendrup established that the GI system adapts with consistent training. A high-carbohydrate diet and regular fueling practice increase the density of SGLT1 transporters in the small intestine - the cellular gates through which glucose moves into the bloodstream. More transporters means faster absorption and less undigested carbohydrate sitting in your gut.

A [two-week gut-training study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28177715/) of 25 endurance runners found that carbohydrate-fueling groups reduced GI symptoms by 60-63% and improved running performance by roughly 5% compared to a placebo group. Two weeks of consistent practice produced measurable results.

## How Much Fuel Does Your Gut Actually Need?

Your carbohydrate target depends on event duration and current adaptation level. For events under 90 minutes, 30-45 g/hr is adequate. For 2.5-plus hour events, the target climbs to 60-90+ g/hr, alongside 500-700 ml of fluid per hour.

For long events, using a glucose-fructose blend (or maltodextrin-fructose) allows absorption beyond the 60 g/hr ceiling that glucose alone can hit, since these carbohydrates use different intestinal transporters. Carb targets by athlete level per hour:

- Beginners: under 60 g/hr
- Intermediate: around 60 g/hr
- Advanced: 75-90 g/hr
- Elite: 90+ g/hr

## The Step-by-Step Gut Training: How to Teach Your Stomach to Handle Race Fuel Protocol

![Runner consuming a gel on a trail during a long training run, practicing in-session fueling for gut adaptation](/blog/assets/gut-training-how-to-teach-your-stomach-to-handle-race-fuel/image_2.jpg)

Start gut training 6-10 weeks before your race, with 1-2 dedicated sessions per week. Long, easy efforts are the best adaptation window because gut blood flow remains relatively high at low intensity.

The three-level progression:

- **Level 1 - Building (weeks 1-4)**: 30-60 g/hr on 60-90 min runs, fueling every 20-30 minutes
- **Level 2 - Adaptation (weeks 4-7)**: 60-90 g/hr on 1.5-2.5 hour runs using glucose-fructose products, mild early bloating is normal
- **Level 3 - Race-Simulation (final 4-6 weeks)**: 90-120 g/hr with race-pace segments, fueling every 15-25 minutes under real race conditions

## What to Practice With: Choosing Your Race Fuels

The single most important rule is to use the exact products you plan to race with. Introducing anything new on race day is a gamble your stomach may not forgive.

- **Gels and chews**: convenient, easy to dose, and available in isotonic formats that require less water to absorb
- **Drink mixes**: useful for carbohydrates and hydration together - watch osmolality, as concentrated drinks slow gastric emptying
- **Real food**: bars and chews suit lower-intensity events and athletes with solid-food tolerance
- **Isotonic beverages**: target osmolality close to blood plasma (~280-310 mOsm/kg) for the fastest absorption rate

Test flavors during training too. Flavor fatigue on long races is real, and a gel you dislike at hour three is a gel you simply won't take.

### How to Gradually Increase Your Carbohydrate Intake

Progression is the key variable in gut training. Add 10-15 g/hr every 2-3 weeks and only increase when the previous level feels comfortable across multiple sessions.

- Start with half a gel or a few sips of sports drink to establish your personal baseline
- Log carbohydrate, fluid, and sodium intake each session in a simple spreadsheet
- Keep a symptom diary noting bloating, cramping, or energy dips after each fueling session

### Training Sessions That Build the Best Gut Tolerance

Easy long efforts form the foundation of gut adaptation. Add race-pace segments only in the final 4-6 weeks once baseline tolerance is established. Fuel every 15-25 minutes rather than waiting until you feel depleted.

Effective session types for building gut tolerance:

- Long easy runs or rides at conversational pace - the primary adaptation tool
- Runs done 1-2 hours after a meal to condition the gut to a full stomach
- Race-pace intervals with active fueling to bridge the gap to race-day intensity
- Hot or hilly sessions that mimic the real stressors that slow gastric emptying

## Pre-Race Nutrition That Protects Your Gut

The 72 hours before a race are as important as any training session. Follow a low-residue diet - low fiber, low fat, easy-to-digest carbohydrates - to reduce gut content and lower distress risk on the start line.

On race morning, allow roughly 1 hour of digestion time per 200-300 calories consumed. Aim for pale yellow urine as your hydration benchmark, and plan to limit total fluid loss during the race to around 2% of body weight.

## Common Gut Training Mistakes to Avoid

![Athlete reviewing a fueling log and nutrition products at a table, planning and tracking gut training progress](/blog/assets/gut-training-how-to-teach-your-stomach-to-handle-race-fuel/image_3.jpg)

Even athletes who understand gut training make avoidable errors that undermine race-day nutrition. The most common:

- **Skipping fueling practice entirely**: treating nutrition as an afterthought means race day becomes the first real test
- **Switching products last minute**: untested fuels introduce unknown GI risk at the worst possible moment
- **Overdoing caffeine**: high-dose caffeine significantly raises GI disturbance risk; test your tolerance in training first
- **Taking NSAIDs or aspirin pre-race**: these can damage the intestinal wall and worsen permeability under race stress
- **Using products with sugar alcohols**: common in some chews and bars, they cause bloating in many athletes
- **Overconsumption on race day**: stick to your tested fueling plan - more is not always better, and exceeding your trained ceiling causes the same GI breakdown as underfueling

## Signs Your Gut Training Is Working

Gut adaptation is gradual, but the markers are clear. Progress shows up as comfort and consistency rather than dramatic performance leaps.

Look for these signs across your training cycle:

- **Reduced bloating and cramping**: fuel sits comfortably during runs rather than causing mid-session distress
- **Stable energy**: fewer bonks and fewer energy dips across long efforts
- **Higher carb tolerance**: you reach your target g/hr without the symptoms that stopped you before
- **Predictable bathroom patterns**: gut behavior becomes consistent and foreseeable before and during races

The [IRONMAN gut training guide](https://www.ironman.com/training/nutrition/fueling-training/how-train-your-gut-race-day) recommends monthly race-simulation sessions as a check-in tool - run your exact fueling plan and log every variable. If your intake numbers are climbing and your symptoms are dropping, the training is working exactly as it should.
