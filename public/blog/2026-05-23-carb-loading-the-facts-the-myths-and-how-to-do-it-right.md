# Carb Loading: The Facts, the Myths, and How to Do It Right

If you've ever eaten a heaping plate of pasta the night before a race and called it a strategy, you're not alone, but you're also not quite there yet. **Carb loading** confuses even experienced runners, largely because outdated advice from the 1960s and 70s still circulates in training groups and old guidebooks.

![Runner preparing pre-race carbohydrate meal with pasta, rice, and bread spread across a table](/blog/assets/carb-loading-the-facts-the-myths-and-how-to-do-it-right/image_1.jpg)

The core idea is straightforward: maximize the glycogen stored in your muscles before a long endurance event so you have more fuel available when it counts. Modern research has simplified the process considerably. A focused 24 to 48 hour window of high-carbohydrate eating, paired with a training taper, is enough to fully saturate glycogen stores for most runners.

## What Is Carb Loading and Why Does It Matter for Runners?

Glycogen is the stored form of carbohydrate in your body and the primary fuel your muscles draw on during sustained, moderate-to-high intensity running. Your muscles and liver together hold roughly 400 to 500 grams of glycogen, but this supply is finite. For most runners, those stores sustain about 75 to 90 minutes of hard effort before running critically low.

- **Liver glycogen** regulates blood glucose and keeps your brain fueled
- **Muscle glycogen** powers the working muscles directly
- **Who benefits most:** runners whose event lasts longer than 60 to 90 minutes, so half-marathoners (depending on pace) and all marathon runners

For 5K and 10K runners, normal daily eating is enough.

## The Science Behind Glycogen and Endurance Performance

When glycogen runs low, your body shifts toward fat oxidation, which is slower and less efficient at race pace. The result is the familiar "hitting the wall" sensation, a sudden drop in pace and energy that typically strikes around mile 18 to 20 in a marathon.

Carb loading can increase glycogen stores by 30 to 50 percent above baseline, directly delaying that depletion point. There is also a water component worth understanding: each gram of glycogen stored in muscle binds approximately 3 grams of water. A well-loaded runner may gain 1 to 2 kilograms before a race. That weight is fuel and the fluid that comes with it, and it dissipates during the race as glycogen is burned.

## Separating Truth from Fiction

**Myth 1: A big pasta dinner the night before is enough.** A single large meal cannot fill glycogen stores depleted across a week of training. Effective loading requires a multi-day window of 36 to 72 hours, with carbohydrates spread across multiple meals.

**Myth 2: You need a depletion phase first.** The original 1960s protocol involved a grueling depletion run followed by days of very low carbohydrate intake. Research by Bussau et al. (2002) showed this step is unnecessary. Trained athletes can fully saturate glycogen stores with just 24 to 36 hours of high carbohydrate intake.

**Myth 3: Carb loading means eating as much as possible.** Once stores are saturated, excess carbohydrate is stored as fat or causes GI distress. The correct approach is to shift your plate composition, increasing the proportion of carbohydrates while reducing fat and protein, not simply adding volume.

**Myth 4: Every runner needs to carb load.** Half-marathon runners over 90 minutes and all marathon runners benefit clearly. Shorter-distance runners do not.

## The Modern Carb Loading Protocol

The current consensus: consume 8 to 12 grams of carbohydrate per kilogram of body weight per day for 24 to 48 hours before your race, paired with a training taper. No depletion phase. Low-fiber food choices to keep your gut comfortable. Familiar foods only.

### How Much Should You Eat?

| Runner | Body Weight | Daily Carb Target (8-10 g/kg) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Amateur | 60 kg | 480-600 g |
| Amateur | 70 kg | 560-700 g |
| Elite/experienced | 70 kg | 700-840 g |

Spread these grams across breakfast, lunch, snacks, and dinner. Check out [Science in Sport's carb loading guide by race distance](https://www.scienceinsport.com/sports-nutrition/carb-loading-by-race-distance/) for pre-calculated targets by body weight.

### What to Eat (and What to Avoid)

**Good choices:** white rice, pasta, white bread, potatoes, sports drinks, bananas, fruit juice, oats, rice cakes.

**Avoid:** high-fiber foods (lentils, beans, cruciferous vegetables), heavy cream sauces, fried foods, and anything new or unfamiliar.

## Your Day-by-Day Schedule Before Race Day

**Days 7-3:** Eat normally and begin your taper. No need to front-load carbohydrates this far out.

**Days 2-1:** Switch to a high-carbohydrate focus, targeting 10 to 12 grams per kilogram of body weight across the day. Prioritize low-fiber sources. Keep dinner moderate and eat it by 6 to 7 pm so your stomach is settled before sleep.

**Race morning:** Eat 1 to 2 grams of carbohydrate per kilogram of body weight, 3 to 4 hours before your start time. Stick to familiar foods: toast, a banana, oats, or a sports drink. This tops up liver glycogen after an overnight fast without overloading your stomach.

## Practical Tips for Race Day Success

![Diagram showing glycogen depletion curve during a marathon with wall-hitting zone highlighted around mile 18-20](/blog/assets/carb-loading-the-facts-the-myths-and-how-to-do-it-right/image_2.jpg)

**Hydration and sleep** are often overlooked. Because glycogen binds water, adequate fluid intake is essential for complete storage. Drink to thirst in the days before the race and include sodium to support electrolyte balance. Sleep is equally important: glycogen synthesis continues during rest, so poor sleep in the loading window can blunt your results.

**Practice your strategy in training.** Do not attempt carb loading for the first time on race week. Use one or two long runs as rehearsals. Load for 24 hours before a 20-mile training run, note how your gut responds, and adjust as needed. [Trekon](https://www.trekon.run) offers structured training plans that build in pre-long-run fueling practice so the protocol becomes second nature before it matters most.

**On-race fueling:** Even with full glycogen stores at the start, a marathon will deplete them. Aim for 30 to 60 grams of carbohydrate per hour during the race using gels, sports drinks, or chews. Train your gut to handle these products during long runs so there are no surprises on race day.

## Common Carb Loading Mistakes

- **Starting too late:** a single night-before meal cannot fill depleted stores
- **Too much fiber:** causes bloating, gas, and mid-race GI distress
- **Not hitting targets:** use body weight targets, not guesswork
- **Trying new foods:** unfamiliar foods in race week are a common cause of stomach problems
- **Skipping hydration:** glycogen cannot be stored without adequate fluid intake

## Who Should (and Shouldn't) Carb Load?

![Runner logging a pre-long-run meal in a training journal alongside a running app screen showing an upcoming workout plan](/blog/assets/carb-loading-the-facts-the-myths-and-how-to-do-it-right/image_3.jpg)

Carb loading is a targeted tool, not a universal requirement:

| Runner Type | Should Carb Load? |
| --- | --- |
| 5K/10K runner | No |
| Half-marathon under 90 min | Emphasize carbs day before |
| Half-marathon over 90 min | Yes, 24-hour protocol |
| Marathon runner | Yes, 24-48 hour protocol |

Individual factors including age, body composition, training level, and gut sensitivity all influence how your body responds. [Tufts University's evidence-based carb loading guidance](https://now.tufts.edu/2026/04/02/should-you-carbo-load-marathon) notes that nutritional plans should be tailored to the individual rather than applied as a one-size-fits-all rule. If you have specific dietary needs or health considerations, working with a sports dietitian to build a personalized plan is the most reliable path to getting it right.
