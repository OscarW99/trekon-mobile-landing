# Real Food vs Gels: Fuelling Options for Long Runs

When the kilometres stack up and your glycogen stores start to drain, what you eat mid-run matters as much as your training. The debate around **Real Food vs Gels: Fuelling Options for Long Runs** is one every distance runner faces eventually, whether you're preparing for your first half marathon or tackling a trail ultra.

![Runner eating a banana and energy gel during a long trail run](/blog/assets/real-food-vs-gels-fuelling-options-for-long-runs/image_1.jpg)

The good news is that your body doesn't particularly care whether carbohydrates arrive in a foil sachet or a zip-lock bag of dates. What it responds to is how much carbohydrate reaches your muscles per hour and whether your gut can handle the delivery.

For runs beyond roughly 90 minutes, most runners need somewhere between 30 and 60 grams of carbohydrate per hour to stay ahead of the wall.

Both gels and real food can hit that target. The question is which option suits your pace, your stomach, and the demands of the specific run in front of you. This guide breaks down when each approach wins, what practical options exist, and how to build a fuelling strategy you can actually rely on come race day.

## Why Fuelling Matters on Long Runs

Your muscles run on glycogen, and most runners carry roughly 90 minutes worth at race pace. Once those stores deplete, pace drops sharply, concentration fades, and legs fill with lead - what runners call bonking.

For runs under 60 minutes, supplemental fuel is usually unnecessary if you have eaten beforehand. Beyond that threshold, and especially past 90 minutes, mid-run carbohydrates shift from optional to essential.

## How Much Carbohydrate Do You Actually Need?

![Runner taking a gel from a race vest pocket on a long trail run](/blog/assets/real-food-vs-gels-fuelling-options-for-long-runs/image_2.jpg)

Most runners should target 30 to 60 grams of carbohydrate per hour. Well-trained athletes can absorb 60 to 90 grams per hour using mixed sources, though that upper range needs gradual practice to tolerate without GI issues.

Timing matters as much as total quantity:

- Begin fuelling at 30 to 45 minutes into the run, before you feel depleted
- Repeat every 30 to 45 minutes throughout
- Combine glucose and fructose sources so absorption does not bottleneck at a single intestinal transporter

## Understanding the Real Food vs Gels: Fuelling Options for Long Runs Debate

Gels and real food are delivery vehicles, not fundamentally different fuels. Your body responds to the total carbohydrate arriving per hour, not the packaging it arrived in.

Cost and GI tolerance are the two practical factors that often decide the choice. Gels are convenient and predictable but expensive per gram. Real food is cheaper and more varied, but digestion depends heavily on what you choose and how hard you are running.

## Energy Gels: Pros, Cons, and When They Win

A standard gel contains 22 to 40 grams of carbohydrate in a compact sachet, typically formulated with a 2:1 glucose-to-fructose ratio to maximise intestinal absorption. No chewing, predictable numbers, and they fit in any pocket.

Gels are the right choice in these scenarios:

- Marathon-pace efforts where chewing would disrupt breathing or running form
- Races under 4 hours that prioritise fast, consistent carbohydrate delivery
- Any session where carrying bulk food is impractical

The drawbacks: cost adds up over a full training block, and flavour fatigue becomes significant after 3 to 4 hours of repeated sweet sachets.

## Real Food for Long Runs: What Works and What Doesn't

Real food works mid-run when it is low in fat, fibre, and protein. All three factors slow gastric emptying and increase GI risk, which is the opposite of what you need on the move.

Reliable options with approximate carbohydrate counts:

- 1 medium banana: approximately 23 g
- 1 small boiled potato with salt: approximately 30 g
- 6 dried apricots: approximately 29 g
- ¼ cup raisins: approximately 29 g
- Pretzels or white bread with honey: 20 to 30 g per portion

Potassium-rich choices like bananas, potatoes, and dried fruit also help replenish electrolytes lost through sweat.

Avoid these options mid-run:

- High-fat foods like nut butter or cheese
- High-fibre choices such as whole-grain bread or apple skins
- Sorbitol-rich dried fruits like prunes, which can act as a laxative at running intensity

## Real Food vs Gels: Fuelling Options for Long Runs - The Hybrid Approach

Most experienced runners use both formats, matching fuel type to run phase. Gels cover high-intensity windows; real food covers the duration windows when pace drops and chewing becomes comfortable again.

A practical ultra structure: gels for the first 3 to 4 hours while intensity is higher, then real food as the dominant source once pace slows and aid stations become more frequent.

For marathon training, a simple rule is gels on race-pace days and real food on easy long days. For [practical guidance on combining fuelling strategies](https://www.enhancecoaching.co.za/blog/running-gels-vs-real-food), the case for mixing approaches is well supported.

## How to Choose Based on Run Type and Intensity

Intensity is the primary variable. When pace is fast enough that chewing feels effortful, gels are the safer option. When pace is easy enough to eat a small potato without discomfort, real food is viable.

| Run Type | Recommended Fuel | Key Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Easy long run | Real food | Lower GI stress; easy to chew |
| Marathon-pace effort | Gels | Fast absorption; no chewing required |
| Ultramarathon | Hybrid plan | Real food dominates from hour 3-4 onward |
| Sub-4-hour race | Gels | Predictability and logistics |

## Practical Tips for Training Your Gut

![Runner reviewing a nutrition plan on a smartphone before a long training run](/blog/assets/real-food-vs-gels-fuelling-options-for-long-runs/image_3.jpg)

Your gut adapts to increased carbohydrate intake just as your legs adapt to mileage. Runners who build gradually - starting at 30 grams per hour and increasing over several weeks - can often reach 60 to 90 grams per hour by race day without problems.

Start this process 6 to 8 weeks before your target event. Key rules to follow:

- Test every product you plan to race with during training, not on race day
- Increase carbohydrate intake per session gradually, not in one jump
- Practice fuelling on every long run until the routine feels completely automatic

## Quick-Reference Comparison: Real Food vs Gels

The right choice depends on duration, intensity, stomach tolerance, and budget. Neither option suits every runner or every run.

| Factor | Gels | Real Food |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Portability | Excellent | Moderate |
| Cost | High | Low |
| Digestion speed | Fast | Slower |
| Carb predictability | High | Moderate |
| GI risk | Low to moderate | Varies by food |
| Flavour fatigue | High on long efforts | Low |

Decision guide:

- Choose gels if intensity is high, the event is under 4 hours, or logistics are tight
- Choose real food if pace is easy, the event is long, or gel fatigue is a known issue
- Choose both if the run extends beyond 4 to 5 hours
