# How to Train for a Half Marathon on Three Runs a Week

Most half marathon training plans demand four to six days of running per week, leaving little room for work, family, or anything else.

But knowing **how to train for a half marathon on three runs a week** is not just possible - it is a genuinely smart approach for busy or injury-prone runners.

![Runner completing a long training run on a trail, preparing for a half marathon on a three-day-per-week schedule](/blog/assets/how-to-train-for-a-half-marathon-on-three-runs-a-week/image_1.jpg)

The secret lies in trading volume for quality. A three-day plan focuses each session on a specific purpose: intervals, tempo, and a long run. Recovery days in between allow your body to absorb the training stimulus rather than accumulate fatigue.

Whether you are juggling a packed schedule or simply trying to stay healthy through 13.1 miles, this approach delivers results without burning you out before race day.

## Why Three Runs a Week Is Enough to Conquer 13.1 Miles

The [FIRST program](https://www.runnersworld.com/training/a73369853/half-marathon-training-plan-3-days/), developed by researchers at Furman University, proved that purposeful training beats raw mileage. Their 16-week study showed veteran runners improved finishing times by an average of nearly 20 minutes while running just three days per week.

Running more eventually yields diminishing returns. Each additional easy mile adds cumulative fatigue without proportional fitness gains.

This plan works best for runners who:

- Have a base of at least 15 miles per week and can run 8 miles comfortably
- Work full-time, parent young children, or face other scheduling constraints
- Have a history of overuse injuries like shin splints or IT band syndrome
- Have completed at least one half marathon or are comfortable with structured training

## How to Train for a Half Marathon on Three Runs a Week: The Core Structure

Every three-day-a-week plan uses the same three pillars: a tempo run, an interval workout, and a long run. Each session targets a distinct physiological system, so no training stimulus is duplicated.

Spacing is critical. Never run two hard sessions on back-to-back days - insert at least one full rest or cross-training day between each key workout.

On non-running days, consider:

- 30-45 minutes of cycling, swimming, or elliptical for low-impact aerobic maintenance
- A 20-30 minute strength session targeting glutes, hips, and core
- Yoga, foam rolling, or a full rest day for active recovery

### The Tempo Run: Building Your Anaerobic Threshold

Tempo runs push your anaerobic threshold upward, training your body to hold a challenging pace without accumulating lactic acid too quickly. For short efforts of 3 miles or less, target your 10K race pace. For longer segments, add roughly 15 seconds per mile.

Always bookend the effort with at least one mile of warm-up and one mile of cooldown. By the final tempo mile, you should feel genuinely challenged but still in control.

### The Interval Workout: Sharpening Your Speed

Interval sessions use repeated short efforts - typically 400m or 800m repeats at 10K pace - to raise your VO2 max and sharpen running economy. Recover with a 90-second easy jog between each repeat to clear lactate without losing the aerobic stimulus.

A 10-minute warm-up before and a 10-minute cooldown after protect your legs and maximize adaptation.

### The Long Run: Your Most Important Session

![Lone runner on a scenic trail road during a long early-morning training run](/blog/assets/how-to-train-for-a-half-marathon-on-three-runs-a-week/image_2.jpg)

The long run is the cornerstone of half marathon preparation. Run at a comfortable, conversational pace - you should be able to speak in full sentences throughout.

In later weeks, include short segments at your target half-marathon pace to simulate race conditions. Build mileage gradually, never increasing your long-run distance by more than one to two miles per week.

## How to Set Your Goal Half Marathon Pace (GHMP)

Your GHMP anchors every other workout in the plan. Run a 3.1-mile test effort at maximum sustainable pace and use standard pace-conversion tables, or adjust from a recent 5K or 10K result.

Once you have your GHMP, your training paces follow directly:

- Long run: GHMP plus 60-90 seconds per mile (easy, conversational)
- Tempo pace: approximately GHMP minus 15 seconds per mile
- Interval pace: your current 10K race pace

## Structuring Your Training Week: A Sample Schedule

A reliable three-day spread is intervals on Tuesday, tempo on Thursday, and long run on Saturday. This leaves Monday, Wednesday, Friday, and Sunday free for cross-training, strength work, or complete rest.

Flex days are not wasted days - they are deliberate recovery that allows your body to absorb the stimulus from hard sessions. Cycling, swimming, and elliptical keep aerobic fitness sharp without adding impact stress to joints and tendons.

## How Long Should You Train? 8 vs. 12 vs. 16 Weeks

The right plan length depends on your starting fitness and your ambitions.

| Plan Length | Best For | Starting Requirement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 8 weeks | Experienced, time-crunched runners | Can comfortably run 8 miles |
| 12 weeks | Most intermediate runners | 15 miles/week base |
| 16 weeks | Beginners or ambitious time goals | 10-12 miles/week base |

The 12-week plan is the most practical starting point for runners who have completed at least one prior half marathon.

## Pacing, Effort, and Progression Guidelines

Effort consistency matters more than hitting exact splits on every run. For base and easy runs, target an RPE of 6-7 out of 10, roughly 70-75% of your maximum heart rate. At this effort, conversation should feel manageable but not effortless.

For tempo work, climb to RPE 8 - uncomfortable but sustainable. Always follow a hard week with a reduced-volume recovery week.

## What to Do on Non-Running Days

Recovery is where adaptation actually happens. Your muscles and connective tissue rebuild during rest, not during the run itself.

Strength training on two to three non-running days is one of the highest-return investments you can make. Focus on single-leg glute work, hip stability, and core control to reduce injury risk across the full training block.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid When Running Only Three Days a Week

![Runner checking their watch and monitoring pace during a road training run](/blog/assets/how-to-train-for-a-half-marathon-on-three-runs-a-week/image_3.jpg)

A well-designed three-day plan can unravel quickly with a few predictable errors. The most common mistakes are:

- Skipping the long run and substituting extra interval or tempo sessions instead
- Letting schedule shifts push two hard sessions into consecutive days
- Cutting the warm-up and cooldown to save time
- Ramping mileage too sharply after a missed training week
- Ignoring early overuse signals like persistent joint soreness or elevated resting heart rate

If you miss a run, simply resume the plan at the next scheduled workout and move forward.
