# The Six Strength Exercises Every Runner Should Be Doing

Most runners know they should be strength training, yet it keeps getting pushed aside in favor of one more mile. The good news is that **the six strength exercises every runner should be doing** require no gym membership, minimal equipment, and can fit into even the busiest schedule. Research shows that adding even a small dose of strength work can improve running economy by two to eight percent, meaning you cover the same distance with less effort.

![Runner performing a squat as part of a strength training routine for running performance](/blog/assets/the-six-strength-exercises-every-runner-should-be-doing/image_1.jpg)

Think of strength training as your insurance policy against injury. Weak muscles fatigue faster, leading to poor form, compensations, and eventually pain in the knees, calves, or Achilles. Targeting the right muscle groups, including glutes, hamstrings, quads, core, and upper body, helps your body absorb the repeated impact of running and hold better mechanics mile after mile.

## Why Runners Can't Skip Strength Training

Running places enormous stress on muscles, tendons, cartilage, and connective tissue with every stride. When those structures are weak, the body compensates, and compensations lead to overuse injuries. Strengthening exercises condition all of these tissues so they can better absorb and redirect impact.

Two to three sessions per week is enough to see real benefits, and many of these moves require nothing more than your bodyweight and a small patch of floor.

## The Six Strength Exercises Every Runner Should Be Doing

### Exercise 1: Squats

The squat is the foundation of lower-body strength for runners. It targets the glutes, quads, and hips - exactly the muscles that drive you uphill and maintain stride length when fatigue sets in.

- **Beginners:** 3 sets of 10 bodyweight reps, focusing on depth and knee tracking.
- **Advanced:** 3 to 5 sets of 3 to 5 reps with added load for maximum force production.
- **Power variation:** Add a jump squat to train explosive output and improve your finishing kick.

### Exercise 2: Deadlifts

The deadlift trains the posterior chain - hamstrings, glutes, and lower back - which is chronically undertrained in runners who focus only on quad-dominant movements.

- Master the hip hinge pattern before adding load: push your hips back, keep a neutral spine, and drive through the floor.
- The **Romanian deadlift (RDL)** variation keeps tension on the hamstrings throughout the movement and is especially useful for tendon resilience.
- Use a slow 3 to 4 seconds down, hold 1 to 2 seconds at the bottom, then explode up in 1 second to strengthen tendons and improve impact tolerance.

### Exercise 3: Lunges

![Runner performing a reverse lunge on a track demonstrating proper knee alignment and single-leg balance](/blog/assets/the-six-strength-exercises-every-runner-should-be-doing/image_2.jpg)

Running is essentially a series of single-leg actions, so training one leg at a time is non-negotiable. Lunges expose and correct left-right strength imbalances that bilateral exercises can mask.

- **Reverse lunge:** Easier on the knee than a forward lunge and better for runners with patellofemoral issues.
- **Split squat:** A static variation that allows heavier loading and deeper glute activation.
- Aim for 8 to 10 reps per side, keeping the front knee tracking over the second toe throughout.

### Exercise 4: Step-Ups

Step-ups mirror the exact mechanics of a running stride - single-leg push-off, knee drive, and arm swing - making them one of the most functionally specific exercises in this list.

- Use a bench or box at roughly knee height; increase height or add load as strength improves.
- Drive the knee up explosively after each step to reinforce running mechanics.
- Particularly valuable for hill climbing and race-day strength in the final miles.

### Exercise 5: Calf Raises

The calf and Achilles tendon absorb and return more force per stride than almost any other structure in the body. Neglecting them is one of the most common reasons runners develop plantar fasciitis and Achilles tendinopathy.

- **Single-leg calf raise on a stair:** Lower your heel below the step for a full range of motion and maximum muscle fiber recruitment.
- Target 30 reps per leg before adding load; progress to a weighted vest or holding a dumbbell.

### Exercise 6: Planks

Core stability keeps your torso upright and your hips level when fatigue accumulates. A sagging core means wasted energy and increased stress on the lower back and hips.

- **Forearm plank:** Hold for 30 seconds, building to 60 seconds with full-body tension.
- **Side plank:** Trains anti-rotation and lateral hip stability, critical for maintaining alignment on uneven terrain.

## How to Structure Your Runner's Strength Routine

Research cited by [Six Minute Mile](https://sixminutemile.com/post/the-6-best-strength-exercises-for-runners/) shows that sessions as short as 6 to 15 minutes, done consistently two to three times per week, produce meaningful gains.

| Experience Level | Sets | Reps | Session Length |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Beginner | 2-3 | 10-12 | 10-15 min |
| Intermediate | 3 | 6-8 | 15-20 min |
| Advanced | 3-5 | 3-5 | 20-30 min |

Pair upper and lower body exercises back-to-back to save time. Always prioritize form over load, and increase weight only when you can complete all reps cleanly. ## Common Mistakes Runners Make in Strength Training

Even motivated runners fall into patterns that limit their results.

- **Lifting for hypertrophy instead of strength:** High-rep, pump-style training adds mass without the force production gains runners need. Stick to lower reps with heavier loads.
- **Skipping single-leg work:** Bilateral exercises hide imbalances. Lunges, step-ups, and single-leg deadlifts are essential.
- **Neglecting calves and hamstrings:** These are the two most injury-prone areas in runners and the two most skipped in training.
- **Being inconsistent:** Consistency beats intensity every time.

## How to Progress These Six Exercises Over Time

Progression is what separates a routine that keeps working from one that plateaus after a few weeks.

- Start with **bodyweight**, move to **dumbbells**, then to **barbells** as confidence and strength grow.
- Shift rep ranges over time: from 10 to 12 reps for beginners down to 3 to 5 reps for advanced strength phases.
- Add **plyometric variants** - jump squats and explosive step-ups - during race-prep phases to convert strength into speed.
- Include a **deload week** every four to six weeks, reducing volume by about 40 percent to allow full recovery.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Strength Training for Runners

### How often should runners do these six strength exercises?

Two to three times per week on non-consecutive days is the sweet spot for most runners. Schedule strength work on easy run days rather than after hard workouts to avoid compounding fatigue.

### Will strength training make runners bulky or slow?

This is the most persistent myth in running. The low-rep, higher-load approach recommended here builds force production and tendon resilience without adding significant muscle mass. The research is clear: it makes you faster, not slower.

### Can beginners do these exercises without equipment?

![Beginner runner doing bodyweight lunges and planks at home on a yoga mat](/blog/assets/the-six-strength-exercises-every-runner-should-be-doing/image_3.jpg)

Absolutely. Every exercise on this list can be performed with bodyweight alone. Start with the prescribed beginner rep ranges, focus on clean movement patterns, and add load only once each exercise feels controlled and stable. Most runners are ready to add light dumbbells within four to six weeks.
