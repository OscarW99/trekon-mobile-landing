# What to Do the Week After a Big Race to Protect Your Body

Crossing the finish line is a massive achievement, but the days that follow are just as important as the race itself. Knowing **what to do the week after a big race to protect your body** can mean the difference between a smooth return to running and weeks of setbacks or injury. Your muscles, connective tissues, immune system, and energy stores all take a serious hit during a hard effort and need deliberate care to rebuild properly.

![Runner resting and recovering after a big race with legs elevated and a water bottle nearby](/blog/assets/what-to-do-the-week-after-a-big-race-to-protect-your-body/image_1.jpg)

Many runners jump back into training too soon, driven by fear of losing fitness. The truth is, a few days of rest will not erase months of hard work. Whether you just finished a 5K or a full marathon, the principles are the same: rest, rehydrate, refuel, and ease back gradually.

## Why the Week After a Big Race Matters More Than You Think

Your body goes through enormous stress during a race, and the damage does not stop at the finish line. Muscle fibres tear, connective tissue swells, and your immune system takes a significant hit. Cardiovascular fitness does not decline meaningfully after two or three rest days, but skipping proper recovery damages your long-term health and performance.

There is also a mental side to consider. Post-race blues are common after a goal event. Giving yourself permission to rest is a strategic choice that sets up every training block that follows.

## What Actually Happens to Your Body After a Big Race

- **DOMS and connective tissue stress**: Soreness peaks around 24-72 hours post-race, but tendons and ligaments take longer to heal than muscle fibres.
- **Glycogen depletion**: Energy stores can take 24-48 hours or more to replenish fully.
- **Immune suppression**: The post-race window is a known period of increased illness risk. Many runners catch colds in the days following a marathon.
- **Elevated cortisol**: Stress hormones remain high for days, suppressing normal bodily functions.

## A Day-by-Day Recovery Plan

### Race Day to 24 Hours: Immediate Care

- **Keep walking gently** for 10-15 minutes rather than stopping abruptly to prevent blood pooling in your legs.
- **Change into dry clothing** and consider compression socks to support circulation.
- **Treat blisters and chafing** promptly with antiseptic and a blister plaster.
- **Skip ibuprofen** unless medically necessary. NSAIDs stress kidneys already under pressure from dehydration.

For more detail, see this [post-race recovery guidance](https://www.runnersworld.com/training/a60229183/postrace-recovery-plan/) from Runner's World.

### Days 1-2: Rest, Rehydrate, and Refuel

These two days are about doing very little and doing it intentionally.

- Take full rest from running.
- **Rehydrate with electrolyte drinks**, sipping steadily rather than gulping large amounts at once.
- **Prioritise carbohydrates and protein** to replenish glycogen and begin muscle repair.
- Aim for **8 or more hours of sleep**. This is when the most meaningful repair happens.

### Days 3-4: Gentle Movement

![Runner doing light stretching and foam rolling on a yoga mat during post-race recovery](/blog/assets/what-to-do-the-week-after-a-big-race-to-protect-your-body/image_2.jpg)

Light movement is now beneficial, but intensity stays low.

- Short **neighbourhood walks** promote blood flow without stressing healing tissue.
- **Easy cycling or swimming** at Zone 1 effort are ideal cross-training options.
- **Gentle foam rolling** can relieve soreness and help nutrients reach damaged tissue.
- Avoid speed work, tempo runs, or anything resembling a long run.

### Days 5-7: Easy Reintroduction

By the end of the week, most runners can consider a short, easy run if they feel genuinely ready.

- A **20-30 minute run at RPE 2-3** is appropriate. Ignore your GPS pace entirely.
- If you still feel fatigued or sore, substitute a brisk walk and rest another day.
- Any sharp or localised pain in a tendon or joint is a signal to stop.

## How Much Recovery Time Do You Need by Race Distance

A useful starting point is the **one day of recovery per mile raced** rule. It is a general guideline, not a rigid formula.

| Race Distance | Minimum Recovery Before Normal Training |
| --- | --- |
| 5K (3.1 miles) | 3 days |
| 10K (6.2 miles) | 6 days |
| Half Marathon (13.1 miles) | ~13 days |
| Marathon (26.2 miles) | 2-3 weeks |
| Ultramarathon | Weeks, not days |

Your effort level also matters. A fun run at conversational pace requires far less recovery than an all-out race effort at the same distance.

## Common Post-Race Recovery Mistakes to Avoid

Even experienced runners fall into these traps.

- **Rushing back because of social media pressure**: seeing others post easy miles the day after a race does not mean you should too.
- **Ignoring pain signals**: masking symptoms with painkillers delays diagnosis and can turn a minor issue into a serious injury.
- **Drinking alcohol**: it disrupts sleep quality and interferes with muscle protein synthesis.
- **Aggressive stretching in the first few days**: connective tissue is vulnerable and does not benefit from being pulled hard.
- **Signing up for the next race immediately**: give your body and mind space before committing to another goal event.

## Special Considerations: Feet, Skin, and Overlooked Body Parts

![Close-up of a runner's feet being inspected for blisters and skin damage after a race](/blog/assets/what-to-do-the-week-after-a-big-race-to-protect-your-body/image_3.jpg)

Runners often focus on muscles while neglecting the parts of the body that take the most direct punishment.

- **Inspect your feet carefully**: check every toe, nail, and patch of skin. Blisters left untreated can become infected.
- Treat chafed skin with antiseptic and a barrier cream.
- **Check for sunburn** if you raced outdoors. Sun damage accumulates during long efforts when you are too focused to notice.
- **Brain fog and nausea** are common in the 24-48 hours after a hard effort. If these symptoms persist beyond two days, see a doctor.
- For ultramarathon runners, dark or brown urine is a warning sign of rhabdomyolysis and requires immediate medical attention.

## Quick-Reference Summary

| Day | Focus | Key Actions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Race day to 24 hrs | Immediate care | Walk gently, change clothes, treat skin, sip electrolytes |
| Days 1-2 | Full rest | Sleep 8+ hours, eat carbs and protein, no running |
| Days 3-4 | Active recovery | Walk, swim, or cycle at Zone 1, foam roll gently |
| Days 5-7 | Easy reintroduction | Short 20-30 min run at RPE 2-3, or walk if still fatigued |

**Warning signs that need medical attention**: dark or brown urine, significant joint swelling, chest pain, persistent nausea, or any sharp localised pain that does not ease with rest.

The most important mindset shift is treating recovery as part of your training, not a gap in it. Patience in the week after a big race pays dividends for months to come.
