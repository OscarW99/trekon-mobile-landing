# The Growing Science of Running Economy — and Why It Matters More Than Fitness

Two runners line up at the same race with identical VO2 max scores and training logs. One finishes minutes ahead of the other. The gap has nothing to do with raw fitness, and everything to do with efficiency. Running economy is reshaping how coaches and athletes think about performance, shifting focus away from aerobic capacity alone and toward how economically the body moves at a given pace.

![Runner in mid-stride showing efficient running form and biomechanics on a track](/blog/assets/the-growing-science-of-running-economy-and-why-it-matters-more-than-fitness/image_1.jpg)

Running economy measures the oxygen cost of sustaining a set pace. The less oxygen required, the more economical the runner, and the more energy left to push harder or go longer. What makes this science compelling is that economy is highly trainable. Unlike VO2 max, which tends to plateau after a year or two of consistent training, running economy can keep improving through targeted strength work, plyometrics, and biomechanical refinements.

## What Running Economy Actually Means (And Why Most Runners Miss It)

Running economy is measured as the volume of oxygen consumed per kilogram of body weight per kilometre at a submaximal pace. Think of it like fuel efficiency in a car: two vehicles can have the same engine size, but one travels further on the same tank. Elite runners typically consume 180-200 ml of oxygen per kilogram per kilometre. Most recreational runners burn through 220 ml or more at the same pace. That gap is not fixed. It reflects trainable differences in how the body moves and converts fuel into forward motion.

The key distinction is pace versus effort. Two runners can cross the same finish line at the same time while one works significantly harder to get there. Economy explains that difference, and it is one of the most underappreciated levers available to any runner looking to improve.

## Why VO2 Max Tells Only Half the Story

VO2 max sets the ceiling for aerobic performance, but for most trained runners, that ceiling stops rising after roughly 18-24 months of consistent training. Economy, by contrast, can keep improving for years with the right stimulus.

Research from Saunders et al. (2004) found that among elite runners with similar VO2 max values, running economy was the primary differentiator between good and exceptional performance. Two athletes can share identical aerobic capacity scores and finish minutes apart in a race, with economy accounting for the gap. Improving how efficiently you use the capacity you already have is where meaningful gains live.

### The Research Catching Up With Elite Coaching Intuition

Elite coaches have long built programmes around efficiency drills, strength work, and plyometrics, often without a clear scientific framework to explain why they worked. Research is now catching up. A [strength training and running economy systematic review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-023-01978-y) published in Sports Medicine found that targeted strength work produces small to moderate improvements in running economy. Short-term interventions typically yield 2-8% improvements, while long-term programmes can produce gains of around 15%. Six weeks of plyometric training alone has been shown to improve economy by 2-3% with no corresponding change in VO2 max.

## What Actually Determines Your Running Economy

### Metabolic and Structural Factors

At the cellular level, running economy reflects how efficiently muscles produce aerobic energy. Key contributors include mitochondrial density, muscle fibre composition, and how effectively oxygen reaches working muscles. Tendons also play a larger role than most runners realise. The Achilles tendon and plantar fascia act as springs, storing elastic energy during ground contact and releasing it at toe-off. Stiffer tendons return more energy with each stride, reducing the muscular work required.

### Biomechanical Factors

Biomechanics is where economy becomes visible. Inefficient movement patterns waste energy with every stride. Factors that influence economy include:

- **Cadence and stride length**: deviations of more than 3% shorter than a runner's preferred stride length increase oxygen cost
- **Vertical oscillation**: excessive bounce redirects energy upward rather than forward
- **Ground contact time**: shorter, stiffer contacts improve elastic energy return
- **Arm swing**: compact, controlled arm movement reduces rotational energy waste

## How to Build Running Economy Through Training

### Strength Training: Heavy Loads Over High Reps

The evidence points clearly toward high-load strength training as a meaningful driver of running economy. Lifting above 80-85% of one-rep max produces small to moderate economy gains, with effects most pronounced at higher submaximal running speeds closer to race pace. Isometric and lower-load approaches show limited evidence of benefit for trained runners. The practical implication is a periodised strength continuum: building a base of general strength, then progressing toward heavier, more specific loading as a race approaches.

### Plyometrics: The Most Evidence-Backed Tool

Bounding, jumping, and hopping exercises improve economy primarily at lower submaximal speeds, making them particularly useful during base-building phases. The mechanisms include improved neural firing rates, increased muscle-tendon stiffness, and better elastic energy storage and return. Combining strength training with plyometrics produces synergistic effects, addressing both the higher-speed demands of race pace and the lower-speed efficiency that underpins long training runs.

### Biomechanical Refinements Without Overhauling Your Form

Technique changes carry real risk if applied too aggressively. Forcing a non-preferred footstrike or dramatically altering stride length can reduce economy rather than improve it. The most evidence-backed targets for refinement are cadence optimisation toward 160-180 steps per minute, reducing excessive vertical bounce, and addressing form breakdown under fatigue, where late-race efficiency losses are greatest.

## How to Measure and Track Your Running Economy Without a Lab

![Sports scientist observing a runner on a treadmill with a gas analyser mask and laboratory equipment](/blog/assets/the-growing-science-of-running-economy-and-why-it-matters-more-than-fitness/image_2.jpg)

Lab-based gas analyser testing is the gold standard, but practical proxies work well for most runners. A simple fixed heart rate test on the same course every 4-6 weeks tracks pace at a given effort level over time. If pace improves while heart rate stays constant, economy is improving. Additional indicators include perceived effort at a set pace, breathing rate changes, and treadmill assessments with consistent conditions.

## Small Economy Gains, Big Race Results

A 2-4% improvement in running economy translates directly to 2-4% faster race times across all distances, from 5K to marathon. These gains compound across a training cycle and stack on top of any aerobic improvements made in parallel.

![Runner crossing a finish line with a clock displaying a personal best time](/blog/assets/the-growing-science-of-running-economy-and-why-it-matters-more-than-fitness/image_3.jpg)

Economy is the foundation beneath tempo runs, threshold sessions, and race-day performance. Building it consistently through strength work, plyometrics, and smart biomechanical refinement reduces injury risk and extends the window for long-term development. Mileage alone does not produce the same returns. Targeted stimulus aimed at efficiency is what separates runners who plateau from those who keep improving year after year.
