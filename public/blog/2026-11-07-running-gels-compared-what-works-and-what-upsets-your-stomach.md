# Running Gels Compared: What Works and What Upsets Your Stomach

You've finally committed to your fueling strategy, torn open a gel at mile 16, and within minutes your stomach is staging a full revolt. It's one of the most common and frustrating experiences in distance running.

**Running Gels Compared: What Works and What Upsets Your Stomach** is a question every marathon runner has to answer. The wrong gel at the wrong moment can derail a race faster than tired legs ever could.

The gel market has exploded in recent years, with options ranging from traditional thick syrups to hydrogel technology, isotonic formulas, and real-food alternatives.

Each claims to fuel you better and sit easier on your stomach. Some hold up; others depend entirely on your individual gut chemistry.

This guide breaks down how different gel types work, which ingredients cause GI distress, and how to find a formula your stomach trusts on race day.

## What Running Gels Actually Do on a Long Run

Your body carries roughly 90 minutes of stored glycogen before pace drops sharply. External carbohydrates prevent that collapse, and gels deliver them in the most efficient race-day format available.

The chew-free design matters at race effort. A gel delivers 20-30g of carbohydrates without slowing stride or diverting blood from working muscles.

- **Glycogen depletion**: typically hits between 75 and 120 minutes depending on pace and body size
- **Format advantage**: no chewing, consumed at race intensity without breaking stride
- **Portability**: a single packet fits in a shorts pocket or race vest

## The Carbohydrate Science Behind Every Gel

Gels combine glucose, fructose, and maltodextrin, absorbing rapidly through the intestinal wall. Glucose and fructose use separate transport pathways, allowing higher hourly intake than glucose alone.

Glucose saturates its transporter at roughly 60g per hour. Adding fructose lifts that ceiling to around 90g per hour, which is why high-carb gels always blend both.

Osmolality also matters. Hypertonic gels can slow gastric emptying and pull water from the gut when fluid intake is insufficient.

### Why Certain Gel Formulas Trigger GI Distress

Hypertonic gels pull water from the gut lining to dilute their concentration. Without enough fluid, this disrupts gastric emptying and causes cramping within minutes of intake.

Common GI triggers to watch for:
- Excess fructose across multiple consecutive gels, especially for runners with FODMAP sensitivity
- Artificial sweeteners like sucralose, which can irritate the gut at running intensity
- Insufficient water: less than 4-6 oz per standard gel consistently raises distress risk

### How the Three Main Gel Technologies Differ

![Three running gel packet types - standard, isotonic, and hydrogel - arranged side by side on a flat surface](/blog/assets/running-gels-compared-what-works-and-what-upsets-your-stomach/image_2.jpg)

Standard gels are hypertonic concentrates that need 4-6 oz of water to absorb efficiently. They are the most affordable and work well for most runners who stay hydrated.

Isotonic gels, like SiS GO, are pre-diluted to match blood concentration. That makes them practical for hot races or courses with unpredictable aid station spacing.

Hydrogel technology encapsulates carbohydrates in an alginate-pectin matrix that passes through the stomach intact. This reduces osmotic stress and tends to produce fewer GI symptoms at higher hourly carb intakes.

## The Hydration Factor Most Runners Ignore

The 4-6 oz water rule per standard gel is not optional. It is the mechanism that enables proper absorption and prevents the gel from drawing fluid out of your gut lining. Skip it and even a well-tolerated formula becomes a problem.

Dehydration slows gastric emptying independently of gel type, compounding every GI risk. Total fluid intake should be 8-24 oz per hour, adjusted for sweat rate and conditions. Isotonic and hydrogel options reduce fluid dependency but do not eliminate it.

## Caffeine and Hidden Ingredients That Affect Tolerance

Caffeine improves performance but can irritate the gut later in a race when blood flow is already reduced. Alternating caffeinated and non-caffeinated gels limits total intake while preserving the performance benefit.

Three overlooked tolerance factors:
- Thickening agents: xanthan gum and gellan gum can trigger reactions in runners with pre-existing gut sensitivity
- Artificial colors and synthetic flavorings: individual triggers worth eliminating if you react to multiple brands
- Electrolytes in gels: useful for combined fueling, but doubling up with a separate source can push sodium too high

## How to Train Your Gut to Tolerate Gels

![Runner's hand holding an open energy gel packet during a training run on a trail](/blog/assets/running-gels-compared-what-works-and-what-upsets-your-stomach/image_3.jpg)

Gut tolerance is trainable. Systematic fueling practice over four to six weeks converts most early gel problems into race-day reliability.

Build up using this progression:
- Week 1: start with half a gel plus 6 oz water at 45 minutes into a long run
- Week 2: take a full gel at race-pace timing, then check comfort post-run
- Weeks 3-4: practice your full race-day carb rate across two or more long runs
- Complete at least three full-protocol runs before race day

If cramping or nausea persists across multiple trained sessions, the gel formula is likely the cause. Rule out hydration, pre-race food, and pace before concluding the gel is at fault. Swap one variable at a time.

## Natural Alternatives for Runners Who Can't Tolerate Any Gel

If every gel formula triggers distress, whole-food options can deliver comparable carbohydrates without synthetic additives. Applesauce pouches provide 20-23g of carbs with fast absorption and no chewing required.

Practical natural alternatives by carb content:
- Honey packets: approximately 17g carbs, glucose-fructose blend, fast absorption, needs water
- Dates (one large Medjool): around 18g carbs, fructose-dominant, chewy but portable
- Dried apricots (5-6 pieces): approximately 20g carbs, viable at recreational paces

For recreational-pace runners with persistent intolerance, natural options offer comparable fueling with fewer GI risks.
