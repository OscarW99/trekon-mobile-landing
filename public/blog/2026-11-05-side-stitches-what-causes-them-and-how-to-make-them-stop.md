# Side Stitches: What Causes Them and How to Make Them Stop

You're a mile into a run, feeling strong, when a sharp stabbing pain hits just below your ribs and stops you cold. If that sounds familiar, you've experienced a side stitch. Understanding **Side Stitches: What Causes Them and How to Make Them Stop** can mean the difference between cutting your workout short and pushing through to the finish.

![Runner holding their side in pain during a workout due to a side stitch](/blog/assets/side-stitches-what-causes-them-and-how-to-make-them-stop/image_1.jpg)

Side stitches, clinically known as exercise-related transient abdominal pain (ETAP), are surprisingly common. Research involving nearly 1,000 athletes found that around 70% of runners and 75% of swimmers report at least one per year.

Despite how widespread they are, the exact cause remains unclear - leading theories point to diaphragm strain, irritation of the peritoneum (the membrane lining the abdominal cavity), and reduced blood flow during intense activity.

The good news: side stitches are temporary, harmless, and largely preventable. Whether you're a seasoned runner logging marathon miles or just getting started, knowing what triggers them and how to respond quickly puts you back in control of your training.

## What Is a Side Stitch?

A side stitch - clinically called exercise-related transient abdominal pain (ETAP) - is a sharp, localised pain that strikes during physical activity, most often just below the ribs on one side. It affects all fitness levels, from first-time joggers to elite competitors.

Prevalence data underscores just how common it is. One study of 965 athletes found side stitches affected 69% of runners and 75% of swimmers in a single year, alongside 62% of horse riders and 52% of aerobics participants.

Side stitch symptoms vary but commonly include:

- Sharp or stabbing pain below the ribs, usually on the right side
- Cramping, dull aching, or a pulling sensation in the abdomen
- Pain at the tip of the shoulder, caused by irritation of the abdominal lining
- Difficulty drawing a full breath mid-activity

The pain is temporary and harmless, but it can be intense enough to stop a workout entirely. Most cases resolve within minutes of slowing down.

### The Science Behind Side Stitches

The exact cause of ETAP has not been confirmed, and three competing theories remain active in sports medicine research. According to [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/why-do-i-sometimes-get-a-side-stitch-when-i-run), no single explanation covers all cases.

The most popular theory is **diaphragmatic ischemia**: intense exercise redirects blood away from the diaphragm, starving it of oxygen and triggering spasm. A second hypothesis centres on the **parietal peritoneum** - the membrane lining the abdominal cavity. During exercise, peritoneal fluid changes viscosity, causing the two membrane layers to rub against each other and produce localised pain.

A third theory involves tension on **visceral ligaments** connecting the liver and spleen to the diaphragm, amplified by high-impact movement. Most researchers agree the condition is multifactorial, with different triggers activating different mechanisms.

### Common Triggers to Know

Understanding what provokes a side stitch lets you manage risk before each session. The most common culprits include:

- **Large meals or sugary drinks** consumed within 2 hours of exercise, diverting blood to digestion
- **Electrolyte imbalances** - insufficient calcium, potassium, or sodium can contribute to cramping
- **High vertical oscillation** and poor core control during running, which amplify peritoneal friction
- **High exercise intensity**, especially in younger or less-conditioned athletes who experience ETAP at higher rates

## How to Stop a Side Stitch Fast

When a stitch strikes mid-run, the goal is to lower diaphragm strain and ease abdominal pressure - without necessarily abandoning your session.

### Side Stitches: What Causes Them and How to Make Them Stop - 5 Quick Fixes

Work through these in order until the pain eases:

1. **Slow to a walk** - reduce pace immediately to lower the demand on your diaphragm
2. **Deep belly breathing** - inhale slowly through the nose, exhale fully through the mouth, letting your abdomen rise rather than your chest
3. **Press the tender spot** - use two or three fingers to apply firm pressure directly on the site of pain while continuing to breathe
4. **Overhead stretch** - raise the arm on the painful side and gently lean your torso away from the stitch
5. **Bend forward** - lean slightly from the hips to relieve abdominal tension and reset your posture

Combining deep breathing with direct finger pressure is the most widely cited quick fix. Most stitches ease within 2-3 minutes of applying at least one of these techniques.

### When to Seek Medical Advice

Side stitches almost always pass once you slow down or stop. Contact a doctor if you notice:

- Pain that persists for hours after finishing exercise
- Severe or worsening pain that does not follow the typical pattern
- Symptoms accompanied by fever, nausea, or visible abdominal swelling

These signals may point to something more serious than a standard stitch. [Healthline's guide to side stitch causes and prevention](https://www.healthline.com/health/side-stitch) covers the red flags worth knowing in more detail.

## How to Prevent Side Stitches for Good

Consistent prevention comes down to three areas: what you eat and drink before training, how you warm up and breathe, and how your programme is structured over time.

![Runner performing a dynamic side stretch during a pre-run warm-up routine to prevent side stitches](/blog/assets/side-stitches-what-causes-them-and-how-to-make-them-stop/image_3.jpg)

### Eating and Hydration Rules Before Exercise

Nutrition timing is one of the most controllable risk factors. Follow these guidelines before every run:

- Wait at least **2 hours after a large meal** before heading out
- Avoid high-fat, high-fibre, and high-sugar foods in the hours before exercise
- Sip fluids in small amounts during activity rather than loading up beforehand
- Maintain a solid electrolyte baseline - sodium, potassium, and calcium all affect cramp susceptibility

### Warm-Up and Breathing Technique

Skipping a warm-up raises the risk of a stitch by putting your diaphragm under sudden load. Spend 5-10 minutes on light movement, including torso rotations and side-focused stretches, before picking up the pace.

Breathing rhythm matters just as much. Aim for a steady nasal inhale and mouth exhale, with a slight forward lean from the hips and a neutral spine to keep the core engaged. This reduces the torso movement that aggravates the peritoneum on each stride.

### Side Stitches: What Causes Them and How to Make Them Stop With a Smarter Training Plan

Long-term prevention means building the fitness and stability to handle running demands without triggering ETAP. Focus on:

- **Core strength** - bear planks, side planks, and Palloff presses strengthen the transverse abdominis, reducing excessive torso movement mid-run
- **Gradual intensity progression** - increasing mileage or pace too quickly overwhelms conditioning before the diaphragm and core have adapted
- **Form improvement** - reducing vertical oscillation lowers peritoneal friction; a professional gait assessment can identify and correct overstriding or excessive bounce
