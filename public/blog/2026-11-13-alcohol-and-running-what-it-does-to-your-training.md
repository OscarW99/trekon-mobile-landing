# Alcohol and Running: What It Does to Your Training

**Alcohol and Running: What It Does to Your Training** is a topic every runner should understand, whether you log miles for fun or chase personal records.

That post-run beer is a beloved ritual for many in the running community, but research consistently shows alcohol can interfere with the adaptations your training is designed to build.

![Runner holding a glass of beer after a race, looking fatigued during recovery](/blog/assets/alcohol-and-running-what-it-does-to-your-training/image_1.jpg)

From dehydration and disrupted sleep to suppressed muscle protein synthesis, alcohol touches nearly every pillar of athletic recovery. Studies show that drinking after exercise can reduce muscle repair rates by up to 37%, and even moderate intake can fragment sleep, blunt hormonal responses, and leave you feeling sluggish on your next run.

The good news is that understanding how alcohol affects your body gives you the power to make smarter choices. This guide breaks down the science clearly, so you can decide what role, if any, alcohol plays in your running life.

## Alcohol and Running: What It Does to Your Training - The Core Science

When you drink, your liver prioritises metabolising ethanol above all else. This pushes fat and carbohydrate metabolism into the background, reducing the energy available for muscle function and glycogen restoration.

Ethanol also acts as a central nervous system depressant, slowing neural signalling and impairing fine motor coordination. Effects are dose-dependent and vary by body weight, sex, and individual tolerance.

### How Alcohol Affects Your Running Performance Directly

Even moderate alcohol intake raises your perceived effort, meaning a comfortable pace starts to feel harder than it should. Your heart rate climbs, aerobic capacity dips, and your ability to hold form deteriorates.

Research shows middle-distance events are most adversely affected by alcohol, with performance declining as blood alcohol concentration increases. Sprint events show clear degradation from 200 m upward.

- Endurance performance declines at blood alcohol concentrations around 20 mmol/L
- Reaction time and coordination suffer even at low doses
- Anaerobic capacity is impaired, limiting your ability to hit PR-pace efforts
- Perceived exertion rises, making moderate runs feel disproportionately hard

### The Dehydration Problem Every Runner Needs to Know

Alcohol is a diuretic. It suppresses the hormone that signals your kidneys to retain fluid, pushing you to urinate more and lose electrolytes faster than you replace them.

This matters most when you are already sweat-depleted after a run. Drinking post-run while dehydrated accelerates fluid loss and leaves muscles under-perfused on your next session.

- Pre-run drinking compounds dehydration before you even start sweating
- Post-run drinking when already fluid-depleted creates a compounding deficit
- Reduced blood flow to muscles slows nutrient and oxygen delivery

## How Alcohol Disrupts Post-Run Recovery

![Runner resting with legs elevated after a long run, with an ice pack on the knee and a fatigued expression](/blog/assets/alcohol-and-running-what-it-does-to-your-training/image_2.jpg)

The window after a hard run is when your body does its most important repair work. Alcohol interrupts this process at the cellular level, suppressing muscle protein synthesis (MPS), the mechanism that rebuilds damaged muscle fibres stronger than before.

Research published in [PLoS ONE](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24533082/) found that alcohol reduced MPS by approximately 24% when combined with protein, and by around 37% when combined with carbohydrate alone. Consuming protein alongside alcohol did not fully offset the blunting effect.

Alcohol also inhibits mTOR signalling, the molecular switch that drives anabolic adaptation, meaning training stimuli produce fewer structural gains.

### Sleep Disruption: The Hidden Recovery Killer

Alcohol may help you fall asleep faster, but the sleep you get is lower quality. It fragments REM and deep sleep stages, exactly the phases where growth hormone is released and physical restoration occurs.

Poor sleep raises resting heart rate and suppresses heart rate variability (HRV), two markers coaches use to gauge readiness. Consistently disrupted sleep creates a recovery debt that compounds across a training block.

- REM sleep is shortened, reducing cognitive restoration and motor learning
- Deep sleep fragmentation blunts growth hormone release overnight
- HRV drops measurably after even moderate drinking, signalling under-recovery

### Hormonal and Muscular Effects of Alcohol on Runners

Alcohol suppresses testosterone production in men, reducing one of the primary hormonal drivers of muscle repair and adaptation. It can also increase circulating oestrogen, further disrupting the hormonal environment for muscle growth in both sexes.

[The ACSM notes](https://acsm.org/alcohol-and-exercise-performance/) that alcohol inhibits growth hormone secretion and is linked to calcium loss, raising long-term bone stress injury risk. This is a serious concern for runners who repeatedly load their skeletons in training.

## Alcohol Before a Race: What the Research Says

There is no ergogenic benefit to drinking alcohol before a race. Every mechanism points the other direction: impaired hydration, disrupted sleep, reduced heat tolerance, and blunted mental sharpness.

The practical guidance from coaches and sports scientists is consistent: avoid alcohol for at least 72 hours before a goal race. This allows hydration, sleep quality, and hormonal function to normalise before the start line.

## Alcohol and Running: What It Does to Your Training Over Time

Single-session effects compound when drinking becomes a regular habit around training. Chronic alcohol use gradually blunts the adaptations your body is trying to build, week after week.

- Training adaptation slows as repeated MPS suppression limits structural gains
- Injury risk rises from compromised coordination and reduced bone mineral density
- Calorie surplus risk increases, particularly from food choices made after drinking
- Motivation and training adherence suffer from recurring fatigue and poor sleep

## How to Minimise the Impact If You Do Drink

![Glass of water placed alongside a beer at an outdoor social running event](/blog/assets/alcohol-and-running-what-it-does-to-your-training/image_3.jpg)

If you choose to drink, strategy matters. Timing, quantity, and what you eat around alcohol all influence how much damage it does to your recovery and performance.

The most evidence-backed threshold for minimal performance impact is approximately 0.5 g of alcohol per kilogram of body weight, roughly one to two standard drinks for most adults.

**Practical strategies to limit the impact:**

- Alternate each alcoholic drink with a full glass of water to slow fluid loss
- Eat a protein-rich meal before or alongside alcohol to partially buffer MPS suppression
- Avoid drinking after hard interval sessions, long runs, or key quality workouts
- Keep intake at or below the 0.5 g/kg body weight threshold when training is a priority
- Schedule drinking on rest days rather than the night before a quality session

---

## Key Takeaways for Runners Who Drink

Alcohol and running can coexist, but alcohol creates measurable costs at every stage of the training process, from performance to recovery to long-term adaptation. The question is not whether it has an effect, but how large a price you are willing to pay.

Individual tolerance varies, so tracking your own sleep and HRV on nights you drink can reveal your personal threshold.

**Practical rules to take forward:**

- Avoid alcohol for at least 72 hours before any goal race or time trial
- Never drink immediately after a hard session - protect the recovery window
- Prioritise protein and carbohydrate replenishment before introducing alcohol post-run
- Track your HRV and sleep on nights you drink to see your individual response
- Reserve drinking for rest days or low-priority social events, not before key sessions

The long-term mindset matters most. Small, consistent adjustments to timing and quantity tend to produce better results than rigid rules that eventually break down.
