# Black Toenails: Why Runners Get Them and How to Prevent It

You finish a long run, peel off your socks, and find a dark, bruised toenail staring back at you. If this sounds familiar, you're not alone.

**Black Toenails: Why Runners Get Them and How to Prevent It** is one of the most searched topics in running health. The condition, clinically known as a subungual hematoma, affects distance runners of all experience levels.

![Runner removing shoe to reveal a black toenail after a long run](/blog/assets/black-toenails-why-runners-get-them-and-how-to-prevent-it/image_1.jpg)

The cause comes down to repetitive trauma. With every stride, your toes slide forward and press against the inside of your shoe, gradually rupturing tiny blood vessels beneath the nail.

The result is pooled blood that turns the nail dark purple or black. It can range from a painless discoloration to a throbbing, blister-like injury that forces you to cut back your training.

The good news is that runner's toe is largely preventable. The right shoe fit, proper sock choice, smart lacing, and a few simple nail-care habits go a long way toward keeping your toenails healthy - no matter how many miles you log.

## What Is Runner's Toe?

Runner's toe is the common name for a **subungual hematoma** - a pool of blood trapped beneath the toenail. Tiny blood vessels rupture under repeated pressure, and the dried blood turns the nail dark purple or black. The big toe and second toe are the most frequently affected digits.

## Black Toenails: Why Runners Get Them and How to Prevent It - The Root Causes

The injury is cumulative, not sudden. Thousands of steps add up, each one pushing the toe forward into the toe box, until the blood vessels finally give way.

Common contributing factors include:

- Shoes that are too short, too tight, or too wide in the toe box
- Long toenails that press directly against the shoe with every stride
- Moisture and heat buildup that cause socks to slip and friction to increase
- High-mileage training cycles and marathon preparation

Gravity also pulls the entire foot forward on descents, intensifying toe-box impact with each step. Shortening your stride and controlling descent speed reduces this force significantly.

### Who Is Most at Risk?

- Long-distance and marathon runners logging 30-plus miles per week
- Trail runners navigating repeated steep descents
- Runners with a wide forefoot, bunions, or a second toe longer than the big toe

## Symptoms and What to Expect

![Close-up of a runner's foot showing dark purple and black discoloration under the toenail](/blog/assets/black-toenails-why-runners-get-them-and-how-to-prevent-it/image_2.jpg)

Discoloration ranges from a faint purple tint to a fully black nail, depending on how much blood has pooled. Mild cases cause zero pain; more severe ones produce throbbing pressure that worsens when you press on the nail.

As the injury progresses, you may notice:

- A blood blister forming between the nail plate and nail bed
- The nail beginning to loosen and separate from the bed
- The nail detaching completely, with a new nail growing slowly underneath

Full nail regrowth takes several months, since toenails grow only about 1-2 mm per month.

### When to See a Doctor

Most subungual hematomas resolve without treatment. Seek professional care if you notice:

- Pain that persists or worsens after a few days of rest
- Spreading redness, warmth, or discharge suggesting infection
- A large, tense hematoma that may need professional drainage for relief

In severe cases, a podiatrist may remove a badly damaged nail to allow the new nail to grow in cleanly.

## Black Toenails: Why Runners Get Them and How to Prevent It - 7 Prevention Strategies

A few targeted adjustments to your gear and routine eliminate most of the risk.

### 1. Get the Right Shoe Fit

Leave roughly one thumb-width (12-15 mm) of space between your longest toe and the end of the shoe. Have feet measured at the end of the day, when they are at their largest. Prioritize toe box width and vertical depth, not just length.

### 2. Master Your Lacing Technique

A runner's loop, also called a lace-lock, anchors the heel and stops the foot from sliding forward during descents. Thread the lace through the top two eyelets to form a small loop, then cross the laces through those loops before tying.

### 3. Keep Toenails Trimmed

Cut nails straight across every one to two weeks, leaving a small buffer rather than cutting flush with the skin. Short, even nails reduce direct nail-to-shoe contact and lower the risk of ingrown nails developing.

### 4. Wear the Right Socks

- Choose moisture-wicking fabrics such as merino wool or synthetic blends
- Look for a seamless toe and a cushioned sole to minimize friction
- Avoid thick cotton socks that absorb moisture and cause the foot to slide

### 5. Use Protective Padding

Silicone toe pads or moleskin applied to the tips of frequently bruised toes add a physical cushion between nail and shoe. This is especially useful for runners with a long second toe or recurring trauma on one specific digit.

### 6. Adjust Your Running Technique on Descents

Shorten your stride and lean slightly back when running downhill. Controlling your speed reduces the forward force that drives toes into the shoe. Trail runners and those training on hilly courses benefit most from this adjustment.

### 7. Replace Your Shoes Regularly

Running shoes lose their cushioning after 300-500 miles. Worn-out midsoles increase impact forces throughout the foot. Use a mileage-tracking app or training log to recognize when your shoes are due for replacement before wear-related injuries develop.

## How to Treat a Black Toenail From Running

![Person soaking a foot in warm salt water to treat a running-related toenail injury](/blog/assets/black-toenails-why-runners-get-them-and-how-to-prevent-it/image_3.jpg)

The first step is reducing the repetitive trauma that caused the injury. Cut back your running load and switch to low-impact cross-training such as cycling or swimming while the nail heals.

For day-to-day care:

- Keep the nail trimmed and the surrounding area clean and dry
- Cover a loosening nail with a sterile dressing to protect the exposed nail bed
- Soak the foot in warm salt water several times daily if the nail detaches
- Avoid painting over a black toenail - polish traps moisture and slows healing

For a large, painful hematoma, a podiatrist can drain the pooled blood to relieve pressure. Do not attempt to drain it yourself, and never pull at a loose nail.

## How Long Does It Take for a Black Toenail to Heal?

Blood under the nail gradually reabsorbs or the nail simply grows out over several months. In many cases a new nail is already forming beneath the discolored one before the old nail detaches.

Avoid picking at a loose nail, which can tear the nail bed and invite infection. For detailed guidance on timelines and warning signs, the [Cleveland Clinic's runner's toe guide](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/how-to-prevent-and-treat-runners-toe) is a reliable reference.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Black Toenails in Runners

### Is a black toenail from running dangerous?

In most cases, a subungual hematoma is harmless. The main risk is infection if the nail separates and the exposed bed is not kept clean and covered.

### Should I keep running with a black toenail?

Mild cases with minimal pain allow most runners to continue training with minor adjustments. If the nail is throbbing or the surrounding skin looks inflamed, reduce your mileage and monitor the nail daily.

### Can black toenails be permanently prevented?

Proper shoe fit and consistent nail care eliminate the problem for most runners. Pair those habits with moisture-wicking socks, a lace-locking technique, and scheduled shoe replacement, and runner's toe becomes rare rather than routine.
