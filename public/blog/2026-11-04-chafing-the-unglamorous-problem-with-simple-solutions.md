# Chafing: The Unglamorous Problem With Simple Solutions

Nobody talks about it at the starting line, but almost every runner has felt it: that hot, stinging patch that flares up mid-stride and turns a great run into a painful grind.

**Chafing** is a skin irritation caused by repeated friction where skin rubs against skin or fabric, often made worse by moisture, heat, and movement. It strikes the inner thighs, underarms, groin, chest, and anywhere else repetitive rubbing meets sweat.

![Runner applying anti-chafe balm to inner thigh before a run](/blog/assets/chafing-the-unglamorous-problem-with-simple-solutions/image_1.jpg)

Chafing is one of the most preventable problems in endurance sport. It doesn't discriminate by body type, fitness level, or experience.

A few smart habits make all the difference: the right fabrics, a proper fit, and a barrier product applied before the damage starts. This guide covers what causes chafing, where it most commonly hits, and the fixes that work.

## What Is Chafing and Why Does It Happen?

Chafing is a friction injury - the skin's outer layer wears down from repeated rubbing against itself or against fabric. It is not a rash or an allergy; it is mechanical damage that builds with time and movement.

Four factors combine to cause it:

- **Friction**: skin on skin, or fabric on skin at a contact point
- **Moisture**: sweat softens the skin barrier, making it far more vulnerable to damage
- **Heat**: raises sweat volume and lowers the threshold for irritation
- **Repetitive motion**: each stride or step adds to cumulative damage until the skin breaks down

A single short walk rarely causes trouble. Problems escalate when these factors stack together - a long run in humid heat, in poorly fitted shorts, with no barrier product applied.

## The Most Common Chafing Hotspots

The inner thighs are the most common site - often called "chub rub" - but chafing strikes well beyond that zone.

| Location | Typical Cause |
| --- | --- |
| Inner thighs | Skin-on-skin friction while walking or running |
| Underarms | Arm swing combined with sweat accumulation |
| Under breasts / bra line | Fabric edge rubbing with each stride |
| Groin and buttocks | Seam and waistband contact over distance |
| Nipples | Shirt fabric abrasion on long runs |
| Feet | Sock seams or shoe edges at pressure points |

### Why Runners Are Especially Vulnerable

Runners log thousands of repetitive strides per session, and each one is a small friction event. Sweat dries into a salt residue that acts as sandpaper on softened skin.

Race bibs, hydration vest straps, and waistbands create gear contact points that build damage mile by mile. What starts as mild warmth at kilometre 5 can become raw, broken skin by kilometre 30.

### How Heat Makes It Worse

Heat raises sweat volume, and moist skin is significantly softer than dry skin. Humidity compounds this by slowing evaporation, keeping skin wet for longer and dropping the threshold for damage sharply.

## How to Prevent Chafing Before It Starts

Prevention is straightforward when you address fabric, fit, and barrier before any activity begins.

### Choose the Right Clothing

![Moisture-wicking compression shorts and seamless athletic underwear laid out before a run](/blog/assets/chafing-the-unglamorous-problem-with-simple-solutions/image_2.jpg)

Fabric choice is the first line of defence. Moisture-wicking synthetics pull sweat away from the skin; cotton absorbs it and holds it in place.

- Choose polyester, nylon, or spandex blends over cotton for sustained effort
- Wear compression shorts or running tights for inner-thigh protection
- Look for flat seams or seamless construction in key contact zones
- Ensure fit is snug but not restrictive - loose fabric bunches and rubs
- Wear a well-fitted sports bra that minimises breast movement

### Apply a Barrier Product

Apply a barrier to every likely hotspot before you start. According to [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/23517-chafing), applying a protective barrier before activity is one of the most effective self-care steps available.

- Petroleum jelly (Vaseline): widely available, effective, and inexpensive
- Anti-chafe balm sticks such as Body Glide: less greasy, longer-lasting, and clothing-friendly
- Zinc oxide cream: good for already-sensitised or reactive skin
- Natural alternatives such as coconut oil or shea butter: suitable for shorter sessions

On runs longer than 90 minutes, carry a small stick and reapply at aid stations.

### Stay Dry and Manage Moisture

- Change out of wet clothing promptly after finishing any activity
- Apply cornstarch or unscented antiperspirant to sweat-prone zones before heading out
- Choose breathable fabrics that allow airflow rather than trapping heat against the body

## How to Treat Chafed Skin at Home

Rinse the area gently with lukewarm water and a mild, fragrance-free soap. Pat dry and allow the skin to air-dry briefly. Never scrub, and avoid alcohol-based products, which delay healing.

Effective home remedies include:

- Aloe vera gel to soothe inflammation and reduce infection risk
- Petroleum jelly to create a protective barrier while skin repairs
- Coconut oil or shea butter for moisturising sensitive or broken skin
- Cornstarch to absorb residual moisture around the affected area

Seek medical advice if the irritation has not improved after three to five days, if you notice pus or spreading redness, or if symptoms suggest a secondary infection.

---

## Chafing Mid-Run: What to Do When It Strikes

Prevention always beats a mid-run fix, but acting quickly limits how bad it gets.

- Reapply lubricant immediately if you are carrying a balm stick
- Adjust clothing, or reposition race bibs and vest straps to change the contact point
- Slow your pace or shorten your stride to reduce movement at the affected area
- If skin feels broken, stop and cover the site with a soft bandage

## Chafing Solutions for Specific Situations

### For Marathon and Trail Runners

![Trail runner applying anti-chafe balm to inner thighs at a race aid station](/blog/assets/chafing-the-unglamorous-problem-with-simple-solutions/image_3.jpg)

Trail runners face more aggressive chafing than road runners because multi-directional movement shifts clothing and pack straps in ways a simple stride cycle does not. Test all gear on long training runs before race day.

- Use an anti-chafe balm rather than petroleum jelly for races: it lasts longer and does not stain kit
- Apply generously to all risk zones before the start
- Plan to reapply at aid stations from around kilometre 25 onward
- [Trekon](https://www.trekon.run) training plans build long runs progressively, giving you time to test clothing, footwear, and barrier products before race-day pressure arrives

### In Everyday Life

Chafing is not limited to sport. Walkers in skirts or dresses, babies in diapers, breastfeeding parents, and long-distance walkers all share the same underlying problem.

- Skirts and dresses: apply Vaseline to inner thighs or wear seamless anti-chafe shorts underneath
- Babies: use a slightly larger diaper, change frequently, and apply zinc oxide cream at each change
- Breastfeeding: keep nipples clean and dry, use soft breast pads, and choose non-irritating bra fabrics
- Long-distance walking: pack petroleum jelly in a small container and reapply before each extended session
