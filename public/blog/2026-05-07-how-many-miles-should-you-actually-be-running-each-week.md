# How Many Miles Should You Actually Be Running Each Week?

**How many miles should you actually be running each week?** It is one of the most common questions runners ask, and the honest answer is: it depends. Your ideal weekly mileage shifts based on your current fitness level, your race goals, and how well your body recovers from training stress.

![Runner tracking weekly mileage on a trail run, checking their watch for distance](/blog/assets/how-many-miles-should-you-actually-be-running-each-week/image_1.jpg)

General guidelines give a useful starting point. Beginners might aim for 10 to 15 miles across three or four runs. Those training for a 5K typically land between 10 and 25 miles per week, half marathon runners often build toward 30 to 40 miles, and marathon runners may peak anywhere from 30 to 60 miles depending on experience. But raw numbers only tell part of the story. How you structure those miles, balancing long runs, speed work, and easy recovery days, matters just as much as the total.

## Why Weekly Mileage Isn't One-Size-Fits-All

There is no universal mileage number that works for every runner. The factors that shape your ideal mileage include your current fitness level, goal race distance, injury history, available time, and where you are in your training cycle. Base-building weeks look very different from peak training weeks, and both look different from a recovery week.

## The Key Factors That Determine How Many Miles You Should Run

### Your Goal Race Distance

A 5K and a marathon demand very different training volumes. Shorter races lean on speed and neuromuscular efficiency, so you can race well on lower mileage with targeted speed work. Longer races demand sustained aerobic endurance, requiring more time on your feet week after week.

As a general guide from [Runner's World](https://www.runnersworld.com/training/a20807021/6-rules-to-determine-how-many-miles-a-week-to-run/):

- 5K: 10-25 miles per week
- 10K: 25-30 miles per week
- Half marathon: 30-40 miles per week
- Marathon: 30-60 miles per week

### Experience Level: Beginner, Intermediate, and Advanced Mileage Ranges

Your training age matters as much as your calendar age. Beginners need time for tendons, bones, and connective tissue to adapt, which happens more slowly than cardiovascular fitness improves.

- **Beginners:** Start with 10-15 miles per week across 3-4 runs. Walk-run intervals are completely appropriate.
- **Intermediate runners:** Typically handle 25-40 miles per week with a structured mix of easy runs, a long run, and one quality session.
- **Advanced runners:** May sustain 50-70 miles per week, built on years of consistent training.

### Half Marathon Weekly Mileage Goals

The half marathon demands real endurance but does not require the extreme volume of marathon training. According to [Runner's World's half marathon mileage guidance](https://www.runnersworld.com/training/a70791803/half-marathon-weekly-mileage/):

- **Beginners aiming to finish:** 20-30 miles per week, 3-4 days of running
- **Intermediate runners targeting around 2 hours:** 25-35 miles per week, 4-5 days
- **Advanced runners chasing sub-1:30:** 45-55 miles per week, 5-6 days

Every level benefits from a weekly long run built progressively to at least 10-11 miles before race day.

### Marathon Weekly Mileage: How Much Is Enough?

Marathon training requires the most volume of any common race distance. Before starting a formal plan, aim to build a base of 15-25 miles per week. Peak weekly mileage targets by experience:

- **Beginners:** 35-45 miles, running 4-5 days per week
- **Intermediate runners:** 45-60 miles, 5 days per week
- **Advanced runners:** 55-70 miles, 5-6 days per week

More is not always better. The sweet spot is where you can train consistently, recover well, and stay mentally engaged week after week.

## How to Structure Your Weekly Miles for Maximum Progress

![Runner doing an easy recovery jog on a quiet road at sunrise illustrating a balanced training week](/blog/assets/how-many-miles-should-you-actually-be-running-each-week/image_3.jpg)

Total mileage matters, but how you distribute those miles across the week matters just as much. A well-structured week typically includes:

- **One long run:** Slower pace, time on feet, placed on the weekend for most runners
- **One speed or quality session:** Intervals, tempo, or a race-pace workout
- **Two to three easy recovery days:** These protect you from accumulated fatigue

Keep 2-3 easy days between your hard sessions. Stacking a long run immediately after a speed workout is a fast route to injury or burnout.

### The 10% Rule: How to Safely Increase Your Weekly Mileage

Do not increase your weekly mileage by more than 10% from one week to the next. Overuse injuries almost always follow sudden spikes in training load. Warning signs you are doing too much too soon:

- Persistent soreness that does not ease after a rest day
- Declining performance despite consistent training
- Poor sleep or unusual fatigue

Every 3-4 weeks, schedule a cutback week where you reduce mileage by 20-30% to allow your body to absorb the training before building again.

## Signs You're Running Too Many (or Too Few) Miles

Both extremes cause problems. Watch for these signals:

**Too many miles:**
- Persistent fatigue that sleep does not fix
- Recurring minor injuries or nagging aches
- Loss of motivation or dread before runs

**Too few miles:**
- Feeling underprepared heading into race day
- Stalled fitness despite months of training
- Struggling to complete long runs without excessive fatigue

Cross-training, such as cycling, swimming, or strength work, can supplement your running volume without adding impact stress.

## How to Find Your Personal Weekly Mileage Sweet Spot

The best mileage for you is the most you can handle while still recovering well, staying injury-free, and enjoying your training. Use these tools to find it:

- **Training log:** Track weekly mileage, how runs felt, and recovery quality
- **Perceived exertion:** Easy runs should feel genuinely easy, not just slower
- **Body signals:** Pain is a stop sign; fatigue is normal

### How Many Miles for a Beginner?

Start with 10-15 miles spread across 3-4 runs per week. Use a walk-run approach if needed. Build gradually over 8-12 weeks before adding intensity or significant volume.

### How Many Miles for General Fitness?

Recreational runners not training for a specific race typically thrive on 15-30 miles per week. Even 15-20 miles per week delivers meaningful cardiovascular health benefits. The most important factor is building a routine you can sustain and enjoy.
