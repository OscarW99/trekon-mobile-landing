# Brick Workouts: What Triathletes Know That Pure Runners Don't

If you've ever watched a triathlete wobble off their bike and find a running stride within seconds, you've witnessed the result of deliberate, specific training that most pure runners never touch. Running after cycling feels nothing like running fresh, and without practice, that gap can cost you minutes on race day.

![Triathlete transitioning from bike to run during a brick workout session](/blog/assets/brick-workouts-what-triathletes-know-that-pure-runners-dont/image_1.jpg)

The moment you dismount a bike, your body faces rapid physiological reshuffling. Blood flow redistributes, muscle recruitment shifts from quads and glutes to calves and plantar flexors, and your nervous system scrambles to reprogram its motor patterns while you're already fatigued. The result is the infamous "jelly legs" sensation that catches unprepared athletes off guard every race season.

Triathletes train this transition deliberately through brick workouts: back-to-back bike and run sessions with minimal rest. Understanding why these sessions work, and how to structure them smartly, can be the difference between a run leg that falls apart and one that actually delivers.

## What Is a Brick Workout?

A brick workout is a training session where you complete two disciplines back-to-back with minimal or no rest, most commonly a bike ride followed immediately by a run. The session rehearses the exact physiological demand of race-day transitions.

The name likely describes the feeling of running on legs that feel like bricks, though some trace it to early triathlon pioneers using the word as shorthand for a training building block. While most bricks focus on the bike-to-run transition, swim-to-bike bricks also exist. The key distinction from regular training: triathlon is one continuous event, not three separate sports. The seams between disciplines are where races are won and lost.

## The Physiology Behind the Bike-to-Run Transition

The moment you dismount, your body begins a demanding physiological reset. Muscle recruitment shifts from the quad-dominant, concentric cycling pattern to the calf and plantar flexor engagement required for running. Critically, cycling involves almost no eccentric loading, but running demands exactly that. Without preparation, the body is slow to adapt, and the "jelly legs" sensation peaks in the first 5 to 10 minutes off the bike.

A runner who steps to the start of a triathlon with strong fresh-leg fitness will still face a shock at T2:

- **Heart rate spike**: Coming off a hard bike effort, heart rate is elevated, making comfortable pace feel like a sprint.
- **No eccentric prep**: Without impact absorption practice, the first kilometer off the bike carries real injury and form-breakdown risk.
- **Effort mismatch**: Perceived exertion is unreliable immediately after cycling, making pacing decisions dangerously inaccurate.

Research supports the cost of skipping this training. Millet and Vleck (2000) identified transition adaptations that only occur through repeated combined efforts. Bonacci (2011) found elite triathletes demonstrate superior neuromuscular control during the run compared to cyclists of equal fitness. Zwetsloot et al. (2022) showed that prolonged cycling measurably reduces running mechanical efficiency, concentrated in the early minutes of the run. The practical takeaway: an untrained transition can reduce run performance by up to 10%, with the highest-risk window in the first 7 minutes.

## The Real Benefits of Brick Training

Brick training is commonly misunderstood as simply "running tired." The actual adaptations are more specific:

- **Neuromuscular adaptation**: Your nervous system learns to switch recruitment patterns faster with each session.
- **Pacing calibration**: Athletes learn what race-pace effort actually feels like post-bike.
- **Heart rate management**: Bricks teach you to interpret heart rate data accurately when it is artificially elevated by the bike effort.
- **Mental confidence**: Familiarity with the transition sensation removes race-day panic and allows athletes to stay patient.

![Athlete in a triathlon transition zone switching from bike gear to running shoes mid-action](/blog/assets/brick-workouts-what-triathletes-know-that-pure-runners-dont/image_2.jpg)

One of the most visible consequences of an untrained transition is postural breakdown. The forward lean and collapsed chest carried over from the aero bike position directly reduces running economy and increases injury risk. Key form targets immediately off the bike include activating the glutes for propulsive running, shifting cadence from cycling rpm to running stride rate, and maintaining upright posture in the first few minutes to prevent the hunchback from locking in.

## How to Structure Brick Workouts at Every Training Phase

Brick frequency and volume should match your training phase. More is not always better, and longer is rarely more effective.

| Training Phase | Brick Frequency | Run Duration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Base | 1x per week | 15 to 20 minutes easy |
| Build | 1 to 2x per week | Progressive, up to 30 minutes |
| Race-specific | 1 race rehearsal brick | Race-pace effort |

Keep the transition window under 90 seconds to generate the neuromuscular crossover stimulus. Sessions under 30 minutes of running are typically sufficient; longer off-bike runs mostly add fatigue with diminishing adaptation returns.

### Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Pairing a long ride with a long run, which creates excessive fatigue without proportional benefit.
- Riding with high power variability before the run, which disrupts transition running efficiency more than steady efforts.
- Repeating identical sessions every week without adjusting intensity or format.
- Treating bricks as pure endurance blocks rather than neuromuscular and pacing-specific work.

### Sample Brick Formats

- **Beginner brick**: 30 to 60 minute easy bike, 15 to 20 minute easy run.
- **Race-pace brick**: Bike intervals at race intensity followed by a 15 to 20 minute run at goal race pace.
- **Multiple brick sets**: Short repeats (10 to 15 minute bike, 5 to 10 minute run, repeated 2 to 3 times) done indoors or outdoors.
- **Sprint vs. Ironman scaling**: Sprint athletes keep total brick time under 90 minutes; Ironman athletes use one long race-rehearsal brick per training block.

## How Pure Runners Can Apply Brick Training

Pure runners who want to build fatigue tolerance can borrow directly from the triathlon playbook without ever entering a triathlon. Running on tired legs, whether after a long bike ride or a hard strength session, trains the same neuromuscular adaptability that brick sessions develop. Hill work is a particularly effective tool, building cardiovascular load with lower impact stress while forcing form discipline under fatigue.

For runners crossing into triathlon, [USA Triathlon's brick workout guide](https://www.usatriathlon.org/articles/training-tips/how-to-use-brick-workouts-in-triathlon-training) offers a useful starting framework. At Trekon, progressive programming accounts for the specificity principle: train the exact demand you will face on race day, not a comfortable approximation of it.

### How to Introduce Brick-Style Training

- **Start indoors**: A stationary bike followed by a treadmill run removes logistical barriers and lets you focus on the physiological transition.
- **Begin small**: 20 to 30 minutes on the bike followed by 10 minutes of running generates the adaptation stimulus.
- **Timeline**: Introduce bricks 12 to 16 weeks before your target race to allow enough repetitions for neuromuscular adaptation.
- **Expect a 4 to 6 week adaptation window**: Gains from brick training require repeated exposure before they become automatic.

## Key Takeaways: What Every Runner Should Steal From Triathlon Training

The transition shock that catches unprepared athletes off guard is a solvable problem. Short, well-structured brick sessions deliver high training value without requiring excessive volume or recovery time. The primary mechanism is neural adaptation, not aerobic development, which means even runners with strong cardiovascular fitness will benefit from the specific practice.
