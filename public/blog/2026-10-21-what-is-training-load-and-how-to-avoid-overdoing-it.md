# What Is Training Load and How to Avoid Overdoing It

Understanding **what is training load and how to avoid overdoing it** is one of the most important skills any runner can develop. Training load is the total stress your body accumulates from exercise, shaped by three key variables: intensity, duration, and frequency.

It has two components: external load (the measurable work you complete, like distance and pace) and internal load (your body's physiological and psychological response to that work).

![Runner checking training metrics on a smartwatch while managing weekly training load](/blog/assets/what-is-training-load-and-how-to-avoid-overdoing-it/image_1.jpg)

Getting this balance right is where fitness gains are made or lost. When load is well managed, your body adapts and grows stronger. When load spikes too quickly or recovery is cut short, injury risk climbs sharply.

Research consistently shows that large, sudden week-to-week increases in training volume are among the leading drivers of overuse injuries. Overdoing it is largely preventable by monitoring both how hard your sessions feel and the objective numbers behind them.

## What Is Training Load: A Complete Guide

As defined in a [consensus statement published in Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise](https://journals.lww.com/acsm-msse/Fulltext/2019/04000/Load,_Overload,_and_Recovery_in_the_Athlete_.26.aspx), training load is the stimulus an athlete experiences from conditioning, training, and competition. It shapes every adaptation your body makes, for better or worse.

Load sits at the intersection of three variables:

- **Intensity** - how hard each session is
- **Duration** - how long each session lasts
- **Frequency** - how often you train in a given period

Recovery is the fourth piece. Without enough of it, load becomes overload, and overload is where injuries begin.

## The Two Components of Training Load

### External Load: The Work You Can Measure

External load is the objective output of your training: pace per run, total weekly distance, session frequency, and the distribution of effort across intensity zones.

### Internal Load: How Your Body Actually Responds

Internal load is your body's physiological and psychological reaction to the work you do. Two runners completing the same session can carry very different internal loads.

Common internal load markers:

- Heart rate during and after effort
- Rate of perceived exertion (RPE) on a 1-10 scale
- Levels of fatigue and motivation before sessions
- Psychological stress from non-training demands

## Why Managing Training Load Matters

When load is applied appropriately, tissues adapt, aerobic capacity grows, and performance improves. When it exceeds tissue tolerance, overuse injuries follow. The Australian Institute of Sport notes that large week-to-week changes in volume are among the strongest predictors of injury and illness.

Managing load also protects the fitness-fatigue balance. When fatigue consistently outpaces fitness gains, performance declines and illness risk rises.

## The Key Principles of Safe Training Load Progression

**Progressive overload** is the engine of improvement. Training stress must slightly exceed your current capacity, allowing tissues to adapt gradually without crossing the damage threshold.

**Specificity and individuality** matter too. Running-specific load builds running-specific capacity, but two runners on the same plan will respond differently. A good plan accounts for individual history, recovery capacity, age, and life stressors.

The widely cited 10% rule suggests increasing weekly volume by no more than 10% at a time. Sudden spikes are where injuries happen, so the goal is always to stay just below the injury threshold while still driving adaptation.

## The Acute to Chronic Workload Ratio (ACWR) Explained

ACWR compares your current week's training load (acute load) against your four-week rolling average (chronic load). The ratio reveals whether you are asking more of your body than it has recently been prepared for.

According to the [IOC consensus guidelines](https://cloud.rampinteractive.com/visionbasketballacademy/files/IOC%20Guidelines.pdf), a ratio between 0.8 and 1.3 is associated with positive adaptation and lower injury risk. A ratio above 1.5 is linked to a significant rise in injury probability.

| ACWR Range | Interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| Below 0.8 | Undertraining / deconditioning risk |
| 0.8 - 1.3 | Optimal training zone |
| Above 1.5 | Elevated injury risk |

Tracking your weekly ratio does not require advanced software. Consistent RPE logging in a training diary is enough to spot spikes before they cause harm.

## Warning Signs You Are Overdoing Your Training Load

The body signals overload well before full overtraining syndrome develops. Catching these signs early lets you reduce load and recover in days rather than weeks.

Watch for these warning signs:

- Persistent fatigue that does not resolve with a rest day
- Reduced motivation or dread before sessions
- Declining performance despite consistent training
- Mood disturbances, irritability, or low concentration
- Disrupted sleep or waking unrefreshed
- Recurring minor illness or suppressed immunity

The distinction between functional overreaching (a short-term dip that resolves with rest) and non-functional overreaching (ongoing fatigue, no improvement) matters. The former is normal; the latter requires a genuine reduction in load.

## How to Progress Training Load Without Getting Injured

Keep each increase small enough that tissues can adapt before the next increase is applied. Rate of change matters as much as absolute volume.

A runner jumping from 30 km to 50 km in one week faces far higher injury risk than one building from 30 km to 33 km. Keep weekly changes modest and predictable to stay inside your tissue tolerance.

After a break, detraining begins within days. Both tissue capacity and aerobic fitness decline, so returning immediately to previous load levels is a reliable path to injury. Treat any comeback as the start of a new progressive block.

## Practical Strategies to Manage Training Load

Alternating hard weeks with planned deload weeks prevents accumulated fatigue from outpacing adaptation. A polarised approach - keeping most sessions at low intensity with a few at high intensity - limits chronic fatigue while still driving performance.

Beyond the runs themselves, these factors all influence how well you absorb training load:

- Sleep quality and consistency
- Carbohydrate availability before and after long efforts
- Hydration before, during, and after sessions
- Mental wellbeing and the weight of non-training stressors

## How to Monitor Training Load as a Runner

![Runner reviewing weekly training load data on a GPS watch and smartphone app, showing heart rate trends and session history](/blog/assets/what-is-training-load-and-how-to-avoid-overdoing-it/image_3.jpg)

Modern GPS watches from Garmin, Coros, and Polar calculate training load using heart rate data and time spent in each intensity zone. Weekly load scores and recovery status give an at-a-glance view of whether training is building fitness or accumulating excess fatigue.

A wearable is useful but not essential. A simple training diary capturing these three data points is enough:

- Session duration and perceived effort (RPE 1-10)
- How you felt before, during, and after the run
- Sleep quality and general energy the following morning

## Key Takeaways: Balancing Training Load for Long-Term Progress

Managing training load well is not about training less. It is about training smarter and more consistently. The runner who builds load gradually, recovers intentionally, and acts on early warning signs will almost always outperform the one who spikes volume and breaks down.

Key principles to carry forward:

- Progress gradually: small, consistent increases beat large unpredictable jumps
- Prioritise recovery as part of the plan, not an afterthought
- Monitor both external metrics and how your body actually feels
- Respond to warning signs early, before overreaching becomes overtraining
- Accept that individual responses vary and adapt your plan accordingly

Sustainable performance comes from finding the training load your body can absorb and adapt to, week after week.
