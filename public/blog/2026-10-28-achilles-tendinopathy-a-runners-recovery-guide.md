# Achilles Tendinopathy: A Runner's Recovery Guide

That familiar stiffness creeping into your heel on your first steps of the morning, or the dull ache that flares up a few kilometers into a run, are classic signs of a condition that affects up to 24% of athletes over their lifetime.

**Achilles Tendinopathy** is one of the most common reasons runners find themselves sidelined. Understanding it properly is the first step toward getting back on the road.

![Runner stretching Achilles tendon before a trail run](/blog/assets/achilles-tendinopathy-a-runners-recovery-guide/image_1.jpg)

Unlike a sudden acute injury, Achilles tendinopathy develops gradually as cumulative load outpaces the tendon's capacity to adapt. Research consistently shows that structured, progressive tendon loading, rather than complete rest, drives genuine recovery and long-term resilience.

## What Is Achilles Tendinopathy?

The Achilles tendon connects the gastrocnemius and soleus calf muscles to the heel bone (calcaneus), transmitting force with every running stride. It can withstand up to 12 times body weight during high-load activities, making it the strongest tendon in the body.

"Tendinopathy" is the clinically accurate term, replacing "tendonitis." The pathology involves structural degeneration, disorganised collagen, and a failed healing response driven by cumulative overload. Lifetime prevalence reaches 52% in elite runners and accounts for roughly 11% of all running-related injuries.

## Mid-Portion vs Insertional: Knowing Which Type You Have

Identifying your type is essential because treatment protocols differ significantly.

- **Mid-portion:** pain located 2-6 cm above the heel bone, affecting roughly 75% of cases
- **Insertional:** pain at the calcaneus attachment point, often aggravated by heel compression from footwear
- **Shared symptoms:** morning stiffness, pain that "warms up" in the first 5-10 minutes of a run, and next-day soreness after harder efforts

Diagnosis is primarily clinical, with ultrasound or MRI reserved for cases where a partial tear or retrocalcaneal bursitis is suspected.

## Why Runners Get Achilles Tendinopathy: Risk Factors

Most cases trace back to a load spike the tendon cannot absorb quickly enough. Key risk factors include:

- Sudden increases in volume, hill sessions, or speedwork
- Weak soleus muscle, reducing the tendon's shock-absorbing capacity
- Foot mechanics issues: high arch, flat arch, or overpronation
- Low heel-drop or worn-out footwear
- Rapid surface changes from road to trail or flat to hilly terrain
- Being male and over 40, the demographic with the highest incidence

## Achilles Tendinopathy: A Runner's Recovery Guide - The 4-Phase Rehab Framework

![Runner performing a single-leg heel drop on a step for Achilles tendinopathy rehabilitation](/blog/assets/achilles-tendinopathy-a-runners-recovery-guide/image_2.jpg)

Evidence-based management follows four progressive phases. The guiding principle throughout is pain-adapted loading: up to 3-5 out of 10 pain during exercise is acceptable, provided it returns to baseline within 24 hours.

### Phase 1 - Managing Load Without Stopping Movement

- Reduce or modify running volume and intensity
- Substitute high-impact sessions with cycling, swimming, or the elliptical
- Keep loading the tendon at a tolerable level rather than unloading entirely

### Phase 2 - Progressive Tendon Loading (The Gold Standard)

Eccentric and heavy slow resistance (HSR) calf exercises drive collagen remodelling. Approximately 82% of runners report recovery by the 3-month mark with a structured loading programme.

The Alfredson eccentric protocol: 3 sets of 15 heel drops off a step, twice daily. Straight-knee reps load the gastrocnemius; bent-knee reps isolate the soleus. Progress load by adding a weighted backpack.

### Phase 3 - Building Power and Resilience

Plyometric work rebuilds the tendon's energy-storage capacity:

- Double-leg pogo jumps, progressing to single-leg variations
- Quick-rebound heel rises to train fast-load tolerance
- Gradual increases in hop intensity and ground contact time

### Phase 4 - Return to Running

Begin on flat terrain only and use the traffic-light pain system:

- **Green (0-3/10):** safe to continue and progress
- **Amber (3-5/10):** proceed cautiously; hold the current load
- **Red (5+/10):** stop; review the previous week's training before resuming

A typical walk-run progression: Weeks 1-2, walk 4 minutes and run 1 minute, repeated 6 times. Weeks 2-3, walk 3 minutes and run 2 minutes, repeated 6 times. From Week 3, build toward continuous easy running of 10-15 minutes.

## The Eccentric Heel Drop Protocol: Step-by-Step

1. Stand with the ball of the foot on a step edge, heels free to drop
2. Rise onto tiptoes using both feet
3. Shift weight to the affected leg only and lower the heel slowly over 3 seconds
4. Return to the start position using both legs; repeat for 3 sets of 15 reps twice daily
5. Progress by adding load through a weighted backpack or vest

For insertional tendinopathy, keep the heel at or above the neutral position - do not hang below the step edge, as this compresses the tendon at its attachment point. Full guidance is provided by the [Royal National Orthopaedic Hospital](https://www.rnoh.nhs.uk/patients-and-visitors/patient-information-guides/achilles-tendinopathy).

## Monitoring Progress: How to Track Your Tendon Day to Day

The VISA-A questionnaire (scored 0-100, with 100 representing full function) is the standard outcome measure. Complete it monthly alongside daily self-testing.

Each morning, before any activity, perform a brief load test such as single-leg calf raises or small hops and record your pain score.

- Note the pain level (0-10) and morning stiffness duration
- Apply the 24-hour rule: next-day pain exceeding the previous day's baseline means load was too high
- Reduce the next session's volume if the 24-hour rule is triggered two sessions in a row

## Running Adjustments That Reduce Achilles Strain

Small changes to technique and footwear meaningfully reduce tendon stress during the return-to-run phase.

- **Increase cadence by 5%:** more steps per minute reduces peak Achilles load per stride
- **Favour heel striking:** research shows a heel-strike pattern places less strain on the tendon than forefoot or midfoot contact
- **Use a heel lift or higher-drop shoe (7 mm or more):** reduces the stretch on the tendon during the stance phase
- **Avoid soft or uneven surfaces:** sand and loose trail increase load variability and slow adaptation

## Adjunct Treatments: What the Evidence Says

Loading exercise is the primary treatment. Adjuncts have a supporting role in specific situations.

- **Shockwave therapy:** evidence supports its use for recalcitrant mid-portion cases after 3 months without adequate improvement
- **NSAIDs:** may ease short-term pain but do not address the underlying tendon pathology
- **PRP injections:** evidence remains mixed and PRP is not a first-line recommendation
- **Corticosteroids:** not recommended - NICE guidance advises against injections due to risk of tendon weakening
- **Surgery:** reserved for cases that fail a minimum of 6 months of consistent conservative management

## How Long Does Recovery Take?

![Runner completing an easy flat road run during Achilles tendinopathy return-to-run phase](/blog/assets/achilles-tendinopathy-a-runners-recovery-guide/image_3.jpg)

Timeline depends on severity and how promptly structured rehabilitation begins.

| Severity | Typical Timeline |
| --- | --- |
| Mild | 4-6 weeks |
| Moderate | 8-12 weeks |
| Chronic or recurring | 3-6 months |

Most runners see 60-80% improvement within 12 weeks when following a structured protocol consistently. If symptoms recur after returning to full training, restart the loading programme early rather than waiting for pain to escalate.

## When to See a Professional

Certain presentations warrant professional input promptly.

- Pain persisting beyond 3 months despite consistent adherence to the loading protocol
- A sudden "pop" during activity, which may indicate a partial or complete rupture
- Significant functional limitation affecting walking, stairs, or daily tasks
- Diagnostic uncertainty, particularly if pain is diffuse, bilateral, or located outside the typical tendon zones

A physiotherapist or sports medicine physician can confirm the diagnosis, order imaging if indicated, and supervise a more individualised programme that accounts for your specific biomechanics and training goals.
