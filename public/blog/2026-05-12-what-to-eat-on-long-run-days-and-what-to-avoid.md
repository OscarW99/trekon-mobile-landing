# What to Eat on Long Run Days (And What to Avoid)

Knowing **what to eat on long run days (and what to avoid)** can be the difference between finishing strong and hitting the wall hard. Your body relies on glycogen as its primary fuel source, and those stores start running low after roughly 90 minutes of continuous effort. Without a smart nutrition plan covering before, during, and after your run, you risk fatigue, poor pacing, and a slower recovery.

![Runner preparing pre-run meal with oatmeal, banana, and toast before a long run](/blog/assets/what-to-eat-on-long-run-days-and-what-to-avoid/image_1.jpg)

A carbohydrate-focused meal eaten 1 to 4 hours before you head out, consistent mid-run fueling every 30 to 60 minutes, and a timely recovery meal afterward form the foundation of a solid strategy. The tricky part is knowing which foods support your performance and which ones cause cramping, bloating, or GI distress mid-run.

In this guide, the team at Trekon breaks down exactly what to eat at each stage of a long run day, which foods to avoid, and how to personalize your approach.

## Why Long Run Nutrition Is Different From Everyday Eating

Your body stores roughly 90 to 120 minutes worth of glycogen in your muscles and liver. Once those stores run low, performance drops sharply - a phenomenon runners call "bonking" or "hitting the wall." Long run days demand a deliberate, timed approach to keep those stores topped up from start to finish.

Blood flow also shifts away from your digestive system toward your working muscles during hard effort, which means heavy or hard-to-digest foods eaten too close to your run will sit in your gut and cause cramping or nausea. Getting your nutrition right before, during, and after a long run protects both your performance and your recovery.

## What to Eat on Long Run Days: Before You Head Out

The pre-run meal has one primary job: top up glycogen without triggering GI distress. Carbohydrates are the priority. Fat and fiber slow gastric emptying, so both should be kept low. A small amount of protein, around 8 to 15 g, helps stabilize blood sugar without burdening your stomach.

**Calorie targets by run duration:**
- Runs up to 3 hours: aim for 300 to 500 calories
- Runs longer than 3 hours: aim for 500 to 700 calories

Keep fiber under 5 g and fat around 5 to 10 g to reduce GI risk.

### Pre-Run Meal Ideas (1-4 Hours Before)

- Bagel with peanut butter and a banana
- Oatmeal with berries and a drizzle of honey
- Two slices of white toast with banana and maple syrup
- White rice with a pinch of salt (great for sensitive stomachs)

If you are eating 3 to 4 hours out, you have room for a fuller meal. Closer to 1 to 2 hours, keep portions moderate and choose lower-fiber options like white bread over wholegrain.

### Quick Pre-Run Snacks (30-60 Minutes Before)

Early morning runners who cannot manage a full meal can bridge the gap with a light snack:

- A ripe banana
- A rice cake with honey
- A small sports drink

Keep this snack to 100 to 200 calories. The goal is a small glycogen top-up, not a full meal.

### What to Avoid Before a Long Run

Avoid these in the 1 to 4 hours before you head out:

- **High-fiber foods** - apples, pears, beans, broccoli, and bran-based cereals
- **High-fat foods** - red meat, bacon, cheese, and full-fat dairy
- **Spicy foods** - a common trigger for GI distress during effort
- **Sugar alcohols** - found in some protein bars; they ferment in the gut and cause bloating

## What to Eat During a Long Run

For runs under 90 minutes, water is usually sufficient. Once you push past that threshold, external carbohydrates become essential. Start fueling at 45 to 60 minutes into the run, before you feel depleted. By the time you notice fatigue, glycogen is already critically low.

**Target 30 to 60 g of carbohydrates per hour** for runs longer than 90 minutes. Using mixed sources, glucose plus fructose, can push that ceiling toward 90 g per hour for trained runners.

### Mid-Run Fuel Options

- **Energy gels** - compact and fast-absorbing
- **Chews and gummies** - easier on some stomachs
- **Real foods** - bananas, dates, raisins, and pretzels work well at slower paces
- **Sports drinks** - combine carbohydrates and hydration in one

### Hydration and Electrolytes During the Run

- **400 to 800 ml of fluid per hour**
- **500 to 700 mg of sodium per hour** to support carbohydrate absorption and maintain performance

For longer efforts, an electrolyte drink or sodium-containing fuel helps replace what you lose through sweat.

## What to Eat After a Long Run

The 30 to 60 minutes after finishing a long run is your most important nutritional window. Muscle glycogen resynthesis is fastest in this period, and your muscles are primed to absorb protein for repair.

**Recovery nutrition targets:**
- Carbohydrates: 1.2 g per kg of body weight
- Protein: 0.3 to 0.4 g per kg of body weight
- Ratio: roughly 3:1 carbs to protein

### Best Post-Run Meal Ideas

- Smoothie with banana, oats, and protein powder
- Greek yogurt with fruit and granola
- Wholegrain wrap with chicken, hummus, or tofu
- Pasta with lentil sauce and vegetables

Avoid greasy, high-fat recovery foods like fast food or fried meals, as fat slows gastric emptying and delays the nutrient delivery your muscles need most.

## How Timing and Individuality Shape What to Eat on Long Run Days

No single fueling plan works for every runner. GI tolerance, sweat rate, run intensity, weather, and personal food preferences all influence what works best for you.

[Trekon](https://www.trekon.run) builds personalised training plans that account for these individual differences, helping runners integrate nutrition practice into their long run schedule. The golden rule: practice your fueling strategy in training, never experiment for the first time on race day.

### How to Test and Personalise Your Long Run Fueling Strategy

Building a reliable fueling strategy takes deliberate experimentation over several weeks:

- **Run fuel trials** - test different gel brands, real food options, and timing combinations on your weekly long run
- **Sweat rate testing** - weigh yourself before and after a run to estimate fluid losses
- **Adjust timing and portions** - if you feel heavy or nauseous, push your pre-run meal earlier; if you bonk early, increase mid-run carb frequency

[According to sports dietitians](https://run.outsideonline.com/nutrition-and-health/training-nutrition/what-to-eat-before-a-long-run-according-to-a-sports-certified-dietitian), individual tolerance is the most important variable in pre-run fueling, and what matters most is finding the timing, portion size, and food combination that works for your body.

## Quick Reference: What to Eat on Long Run Days at a Glance

| Timing | What to Eat | What to Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1-4 hours before | Oatmeal, bagel, toast, banana, rice | High-fat, high-fiber, spicy foods |
| 30-60 min before | Banana, rice cake, sports drink | Heavy snacks, dairy, sugar alcohols |
| During (every 30-60 min) | Gels, chews, sports drink, real food | Waiting until you feel tired |
| 30-60 min after | Smoothie, Greek yogurt, wrap, pasta | Greasy, high-fat recovery meals |

**Key takeaways:**
- Start fueling at 45 to 60 minutes into any run over 90 minutes
- Target 30 to 60 g of carbohydrates per hour during the run
- Recover within 30 to 60 minutes using a carb-to-protein ratio of roughly 3:1
- Test everything in training before relying on it on race day
- Avoid high-fat, high-fiber, and spicy foods in the hours before you run
