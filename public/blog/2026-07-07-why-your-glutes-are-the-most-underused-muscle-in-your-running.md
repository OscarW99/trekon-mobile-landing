# Why Your Glutes Are the Most Underused Muscle in Your Running

Ask most runners which muscles power their stride, and they'll mention their quads, calves, or hamstrings. Rarely do they point to their glutes, and that oversight is exactly the problem. Understanding **why your glutes are the most underused muscle in your running** could be the single most important shift you make in your training this year.

![Runner performing a glute activation exercise before a training run](/blog/assets/why-your-glutes-are-the-most-underused-muscle-in-your-running/image_1.jpg)

Your gluteus maximus is the largest muscle in your body, built to drive hip extension, generate propulsion, and stabilize every stride. Yet most runners rely on their quads and hamstrings to carry the load instead. Hours of daily sitting compound the issue, gradually disrupting the neuromuscular connection between your brain and your glutes, a condition sometimes called gluteal amnesia or dead-butt syndrome.

The consequences go beyond leaving power on the table. When your glutes underperform, surrounding muscles compensate, and that compensation is directly linked to IT band syndrome, runner's knee, Achilles tendinopathy, shin splints, and plantar fasciitis. The good news is that targeted activation and strength work can change this pattern entirely.

## What Your Glutes Actually Do When You Run

Your glutes are not a single muscle but a group working together across every phase of your stride.

- **Gluteus maximus** drives hip extension and generates the push-off power that propels you forward, while also controlling deceleration.
- **Gluteus medius** stabilises your pelvis during foot strike, preventing the hips from dropping side to side.
- **Gluteus minimus** supports knee alignment, reducing the inward collapse that leads to joint stress.

Together, these muscles are active at almost every point in the gait cycle. When they all contribute fully, running feels controlled, powerful, and efficient.

## Why Your Glutes Are the Most Underused Muscle in Your Running

Despite their central role, most runners fail to use their glutes anywhere near their potential. The primary culprit is modern sedentary life. Spending six to eight hours a day seated compresses the hip flexors and disrupts the nerve signalling that tells your glutes to fire. Toronto chiropractor Dr. Mateusz Krekora describes this as gluteal amnesia, a state where the muscle is neurologically inhibited rather than physically damaged.

When spinal joints become stiff from prolonged sitting, your lower back and hip flexors compensate, and that pattern carries directly into your runs. According to a Runner's World UK survey, [only 9% of runners regularly train the key lower-body muscle groups](https://www.runnersworld.com/uk/training/cross-training/a69028839/forgotten-muscle-groups-study/) that include the glutes, making this one of the most widespread gaps in recreational training.

### The Difference Between Weak Glutes and Inactive Glutes

Your glutes are almost certainly firing during your runs, but they may only be contributing around 20% of what they are capable of. The issue is not that they are switched off entirely; it is that other muscles have learned to dominate, leaving your glutes as passengers rather than drivers.

Signs of underutilisation include:

- Hamstrings doing the bulk of the work during glute-focused exercises
- Quad dominance through the push-off phase
- A weak brain-to-muscle connection that makes it hard to consciously engage the glutes

Addressing this requires both neuromuscular retraining and progressive strength work, not just adding more reps.

### How Modern Running Habits Make It Worse

Running itself can reinforce the problem. The repetitive, linear movement of most training runs limits the variety of firing patterns your glutes experience. Quad-dominant running mechanics shift the load onto fast-twitch fibres that fatigue more quickly, increasing knee torque and reducing endurance. Alternating between hard training sessions and long sedentary periods compounds the imbalance further.

## The Injuries That Follow When Glutes Don't Pull Their Weight

When the glutes underperform, surrounding muscles compensate, and that compensation has a predictable injury pattern. Colleen M. Brough, D.P.T., director of Columbia RunLab, notes that inhibition and poor recruitment of the gluteus maximus and medius appear as a common precursor across nearly all running injuries she treats.

The injuries most consistently linked to glute weakness include:

- IT band syndrome and runner's knee from increased knee torque
- Achilles tendinopathy, linked to delayed glute activation in a 2014 study in *Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise*
- Hamstring strains and stress fractures from compensatory overload
- Plantar fasciitis and shin splints from altered lower-leg mechanics
- Lower back strain from pelvic drop and instability

Common warning signs during runs include visible hip drop when fatigued, knees collapsing inward, overstriding, hamstring cramping, and recurring niggles that shift location rather than resolving.

## How to Wake Up Your Glutes Before You Run

Activation is not the same as a warm-up. A warm-up raises your heart rate; activation specifically targets the neuromuscular connection between your brain and your glutes so they fire at the right moment during your run.

A practical pre-run activation routine should take no more than five to ten minutes and include:

- **Glute bridges** - focus on squeezing at the top and pressing through the heels
- **Donkey kicks** - effective for isolating the gluteus maximus without loading the lower back
- **Lateral band walks and clamshells** - target the gluteus medius for pelvic stability
- **Single-leg deadlifts and step-ups** - introduce balance and functional hip extension patterns

One practical cue from Dr. Krekora: push consciously through your heels during every exercise. Heel pressure shifts the load away from the quads and directly into the glutes. If activation still feels stubborn, physically tapping the glutes before and during sets can help reset the brain-muscle connection.

## Building a Long-Term Glute Strength Routine

![Runner performing a glute bridge on a gym mat as part of a pre-run activation routine](/blog/assets/why-your-glutes-are-the-most-underused-muscle-in-your-running/image_2.jpg)

Activation work before runs is essential, but long-term improvement requires dedicated strength training that progressively loads the glutes through a variety of movement patterns.

An effective long-term approach combines:

- **Compound movements** such as squats and lunges for overall posterior chain strength
- **Isolation movements** such as hip thrusts and single-leg bridges to target the glutes directly
- **Movement variety** to retrain firing patterns beyond the linear repetition of running
- **Daily posture habits** including standing breaks, hip flexor stretching, and conscious glute engagement

Runners who commit to this approach consistently report improvements in running economy, reduced injury frequency, and better control of their stride under fatigue.

### Tracking Progress and Staying Consistent

Progress with glute training is gradual but measurable. Useful markers include:

- Improved form under fatigue, particularly reduced hip drop in the later miles
- Ability to consciously feel and engage the glutes during both exercises and runs
- Reduced frequency of compensatory injuries
- Increased cadence and reduced overstriding as hip extension improves

Regular form checks, whether self-recorded or assessed by a coach, provide the feedback loop needed to confirm that strength gains are translating into better running mechanics. For runners who want structured guidance, [5 Signs of Weak Glutes and How to Strengthen Them](https://www.runnersworld.com/training/a64687693/weak-glutes-signs-and-solutions/) is a practical starting point.

## Conclusion: Stop Leaving Your Most Powerful Muscle on the Bench

![Runner holding their knee on a track, illustrating a common glute-related running injury](/blog/assets/why-your-glutes-are-the-most-underused-muscle-in-your-running/image_3.jpg)

Your glutes are built to be the engine of your running, but sedentary habits, repetitive training patterns, and a lack of targeted work mean most runners never access that potential. The result is slower times, wasted energy, and an injury risk that compounds over every training cycle.

The solution is straightforward, even if it requires consistency. Combine pre-run activation with progressive strength training, address the neuromuscular inhibition caused by daily sitting, and give your glutes the deliberate attention they have always deserved. Start with five minutes before your next run, and build from there.
