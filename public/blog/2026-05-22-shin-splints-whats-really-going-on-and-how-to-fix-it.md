# Shin Splints: What's Really Going On and How to Fix It

If you've ever felt a dull, nagging ache along the front or inner edge of your shin mid-run, you already know how quickly it can derail your training. Research suggests that between 13 and 20 percent of runners will deal with medial tibial stress syndrome, the clinical term for shin splints, at some point in their training.

![Runner holding their lower leg in pain due to shin splints during a road run](/blog/assets/shin-splints-whats-really-going-on-and-how-to-fix-it/image_1.jpg)

The good news is that shin splints are highly treatable and, in most cases, completely preventable. The less welcome news is that pushing through the pain without addressing the root cause can turn a manageable overuse injury into a stress fracture, meaning months off instead of weeks. Understanding what is actually happening in the tissue is the first step toward a smarter recovery.

## What Are Shin Splints, Really?

"Shin splints" is an umbrella term for medial tibial stress syndrome (MTSS), involving pain along the tibia where bone, muscle, and connective tissue meet under repeated stress. The term is widely misused: stress fractures, compartment syndrome, and posterior tibial tendinopathy can all feel similar, which is why a proper diagnosis matters.

MTSS most commonly affects:

- Beginner runners building mileage for the first time
- Intermediate runners returning after a break
- Military recruits undergoing high-volume training
- Athletes switching sports or surfaces suddenly

## Understanding the Root Cause

The core problem is a mismatch between training load and the body's ability to adapt. When repetitive stress accumulates faster than the tibia and surrounding tissues can remodel, the periosteum becomes irritated and the bone begins a stress response. Contributing factors include:

- **Training load errors**: too much mileage added too quickly
- **Tissue overload**: fatigued muscles transfer more force directly to the bone
- **Non-mechanical stressors**: poor sleep, high psychological stress, and inadequate nutrition slow bone adaptation
- **Biomechanical factors**: high vertical loading rate, excessive pronation, or structural alignment issues

### How Shin Splints Differ From a Stress Fracture

Shin splints and stress fractures sit on the same continuum. Ignoring MTSS can allow it to progress into a true fracture, turning a 2-6 week recovery into a 4-6 month forced break.

| Feature | Shin Splints | Stress Fracture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pain location | Broad, diffuse (5 cm or more) | Pinpoint, localised |
| Warm-up effect | Often eases with warm-up | Does not ease |
| Recovery time | 2-6 weeks | 4-6 months |
| Hop test | Usually tolerable | Painful or impossible |

## Recognising the Symptoms

Shin splint pain typically runs along the inner border of the lower leg, spanning at least 5 centimetres of the tibia. The sensation is usually a dull ache rather than a sharp stab, though it can sharpen with continued activity.

Common symptom patterns include:

- Pain that starts during a run and lingers afterward
- Tenderness when pressing along the shin
- Relief with rest, returning when activity resumes
- Gradual worsening if training continues without modification

## How Shin Splints Are Diagnosed

![Physiotherapist examining a runner's lower leg during a clinical assessment for shin splints](/blog/assets/shin-splints-whats-really-going-on-and-how-to-fix-it/image_2.jpg)

Diagnosis is primarily clinical. A physiotherapist or sports medicine doctor will review your training history, assess your gait, and palpate the shin to locate tenderness. A hop test (12 single-leg hops) is a useful functional screen. Imaging may be ordered to rule out a stress fracture if pain is pinpoint, occurs at rest, or has not improved with initial management.

## The Evidence-Based Treatment Plan

### Step 1: Load Management and Smart Rest

Complete rest is rarely necessary. The evidence-based approach is graded load reduction: cut weekly running volume by 20-40 percent and keep any running within a pain threshold of 3 out of 10 or less, with pain resolving fully within 24 hours of each session. Maintain fitness with low-impact cross-training such as swimming, cycling, or the elliptical.

### Step 2: Ice, Compression, and Pain Relief

During the acute phase, apply ice wrapped in a thin cloth for 15-20 minutes, 3-4 times daily. Over-the-counter NSAIDs or acetaminophen can assist with pain management short-term, but should not be used to run through pain.

### Step 3: Strength and Rehabilitation Exercises

Strength work is essential for both recovery and prevention. A progressive programme should include:

- **Calf raises**: start double-leg, progress to single-leg, then add eccentric loading
- **Tibialis anterior activation**: heel-walking drills along a flat surface
- **Hip stability exercises**: glute bridges, lateral band walks, single-leg balance work
- **Ankle mobility drills**: to reduce compensatory loading up the chain

### Step 4: Return-to-Running Protocol

Before resuming running, confirm no tenderness on palpation and the ability to complete 12 pain-free single-leg hops. When you return, follow the 10 percent weekly mileage rule, prioritise softer surfaces, and aim for a cadence of 85-90 steps per minute per foot to reduce impact forces.

## Biomechanical and Footwear Factors

Structural factors such as flat feet, overpronation, and overstriding increase tibial loading with every stride. Worn footwear is frequently overlooked: most running shoes should be replaced every 500-800 kilometres. If gait issues are contributing, orthotics or arch supports may reduce recurrence risk.

## How to Prevent Shin Splints From Coming Back

Prevention comes down to consistent habits around load, strength, and recovery:

- Follow a structured training plan with gradual mileage increases
- Build a weekly strength routine targeting calves, hips, and ankles
- Incorporate planned rest days and recovery weeks every 3-4 weeks
- Track your training load carefully - tools like [Trekon](https://www.trekon.run) can help you avoid sudden mileage spikes, the most common trigger for MTSS

## When to See a Doctor or Physiotherapist

Seek professional assessment if pain has not improved after two weeks of load reduction, if pain occurs at rest or wakes you at night, or if shin splints keep recurring despite addressing training load. For a detailed clinical framework, the [evidence-based physio guidance from Pure Physio](https://www.purephysio.net/post/shin-splints-in-runners-an-evidence-based-guide-to-recovery-and-prevention) is a useful reference for both runners and practitioners.

## How Long Does It Take to Recover?

Most mild cases resolve within 2-6 weeks with appropriate load management. Moderate or chronic cases can take several months, particularly if training was continued through significant pain.

![Runner jogging on a soft grass trail during a gradual return to running after shin splint recovery](/blog/assets/shin-splints-whats-really-going-on-and-how-to-fix-it/image_3.jpg)

The key variables affecting recovery time are how early load was reduced, whether strength and biomechanical factors were addressed, and how gradually running was reintroduced. Bone adaptation is a slow biological process, and rushing back before the tissue has remodelled is the most common reason shin splints become a recurring problem rather than a one-time setback. Patience is not optional here - it is the strategy.
