# A, B and C Race Goals: How to Plan for a Bad Day

Every runner knows the feeling: months of training, a perfect taper, and then race day hands you a heat wave, a stomach revolt, or a sleepless night.

![Runner adjusting race pace strategy on a challenging course with weather conditions](/blog/assets/a-b-and-c-race-goals-how-to-plan-for-a-bad-day/image_1.jpg)

**A, B and C Race Goals: How to Plan for a Bad Day** is a three-tier goal framework that protects your motivation no matter what the course throws at you.

The idea is straightforward. Instead of pinning everything on a single time, you set three layered targets before you ever reach the starting line.

Your A goal is the dream performance, achievable on a perfect day. Your B goal is a solid result when things go sideways. Your C goal is the floor - a meaningful finish regardless of circumstances.

## What Are A, B and C Race Goals?

The three-tier goal system gives athletes structured flexibility before any race. You pre-define three levels of success covering a range of conditions and performances, whether time-based or process-based, across any distance from a 5K to a marathon.

### The A Goal: Your Dream Performance

Your A goal is aspirational - the result you chase on a perfect day with ideal conditions. It requires everything to go right: pacing, weather, nutrition, and fitness.

- Aim for a personal best or a significant time barrier
- A coach, training partner, or recent race result can help calibrate this target
- It should feel like a stretch, but not a fantasy

### The B Goal: A Solid Result on an Imperfect Day

Your B goal sits just below the A goal and is achievable even when a few things go sideways. It still feels like a genuine breakthrough at the finish line.

- Near-PR territory, within a few minutes of your best
- Realistic given normal race-day friction
- Delivers satisfaction without requiring a perfect performance

### The C Goal: Your Non-Negotiable Finish

The C goal is your safety net - the minimum result you commit to delivering no matter what the day throws at you.

- Examples: finish strong, run negative splits, avoid walk breaks
- No specific time required; process-based targets work well here
- A meaningful accomplishment regardless of circumstances

## A, B and C Race Goals: How to Plan for a Bad Day

Having three goals in your back pocket directly reduces the fear of failure that plagues single-target racing. When one tier becomes unreachable, you still have forward momentum and a clear objective.

This layered motivation keeps you engaged in the race instead of mentally checking out after missing a split.

![Runner pushing through fatigue in wet race conditions, using A, B and C race goals to maintain focus and motivation](/blog/assets/a-b-and-c-race-goals-how-to-plan-for-a-bad-day/image_2.jpg)

### Common Bad-Day Scenarios Every Runner Faces

Any of these can derail your A goal before the halfway mark:

- Heat, humidity, or unexpected headwinds
- GI distress, cramping, or onset of illness
- Poor sleep the night before, a harder course than expected, or an early pacing error

Having a B and C goal pre-loaded means you pivot without panic rather than spiralling into a negative mindset.

## How to Ground Your Goals in Real Training Data

As coaches at [Sunrise Run Co](https://sunriserunco.com/setting-proper-race-goals/) put it, a race goal has to come from your training. Your watch data and long run paces are honest evidence; race-week excitement is not.

When the goal and the training agree, you start the race with genuine confidence rather than hope.

### When to Set and When to Reassess Your Goals

- **Start of the training block**: set your A goal to guide the shape and intensity of your preparation
- **Early season**: use tune-up races to calibrate whether the A goal still fits your current fitness
- **1-2 days before race day**: do a final check against training paces and conditions; if preparation underperformed, commit to B or C instead

Adjusting before race day preserves motivation and protects the race experience.

## How to Execute A, B and C Race Goals on Race Day

Knowing your three goals in advance is useful. Having a clear execution strategy for each scenario is what actually keeps you running well when things change.

### Starting Smart: Which Goal to Lead With

The most common approach is to begin at B pace and escalate only when conditions confirm it. This protects your energy reserves in the early miles.

- Starting at B pace conserves glycogen and avoids an early blow-up
- Escalate to A pace after the halfway mark if you feel genuinely strong
- Never lead with A pace on a hot, windy, or unfamiliar course

### A, B and C Race Goals: How to Plan for a Bad Day Mid-Race

The moment you recognise a bad patch - a rising heart rate, a stomach cramp, a pace that feels unsustainable - is the moment to act. Early recognition prevents a slow deterioration.

- Acknowledge the signal without catastrophising it
- Mentally reset by shifting focus to the next landmark, aid station, or kilometre marker
- Sliding to the B goal is a tactical decision, not a failure

### When - and How - to Drop to the C Goal

Dropping to the C goal is appropriate when effort has clearly decoupled from pace and continuing at B goal intensity risks injury or complete collapse.

- Assess effort vs pace honestly, not emotionally
- Commit fully to the C goal once you decide; half-measures create the worst races
- The finish line still counts, and a C goal finish is always better than a DNF

## Beyond the Clock: Non-Time Goals Worth Including

![Runner crossing the finish line looking satisfied after a challenging race, showing the reward of having planned A, B and C race goals in advance](/blog/assets/a-b-and-c-race-goals-how-to-plan-for-a-bad-day/image_3.jpg)

Not every meaningful race goal involves a clock. Experience goals, execution goals, and learning goals all deserve a place in your three-tier framework.

- **Experience goals**: enjoy the course, soak in a destination race, run with a friend from start to finish
- **Execution goals**: stick to your nutrition plan, run the first half conservatively, achieve negative splits
- **Learning goals**: first race at a new distance, first event back from injury, testing a new fuelling strategy

Including at least one non-time goal in your C tier makes that safety net nearly impossible to miss.

---

## Setting A, B and C Goals Across Your Race Season

The A/B/C framework also applies to how you prioritise your race calendar. Plan 1-3 A races per year, spaced roughly 12-16 weeks apart to allow complete training cycles and full recovery.

- **A races**: full taper, peak fitness, maximum effort - your priority events
- **B races**: 2-4 per year as tune-ups; race hard without a full taper, at roughly 85-90% of peak form
- **C races**: treat as training runs, fun events, or early-season fitness checks

Spreading your races this way prevents burnout and ensures you arrive at each A race genuinely fresh.

---

## Key Takeaways for Planning Your Next Race

Writing your three goals down is the single most important step. A goal that lives only in your head shifts every time confidence or fatigue changes your perspective.

- Set goals at the start of the training block, not during race week
- Base each tier on training evidence: long run paces, workout results, and watch data
- Reassess 1-2 days before the race and adjust to B or C if training underperformed
- Start at B pace on race day; escalate to A only when conditions confirm it
- If something goes wrong mid-race, pivot early and commit fully to the next tier down

A bad day does not have to be a wasted day. With three clear goals in place, every finish line means something.
