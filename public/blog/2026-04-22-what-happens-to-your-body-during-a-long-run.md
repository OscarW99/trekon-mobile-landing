# What Happens to Your Body During a Long Run

Understanding **what happens to your body during a long run** is one of the most useful things any runner can know. From the moment you take your first stride, your heart rate climbs, your breathing deepens, and your muscles begin drawing on stored energy. It is a full-body effort, and the science behind it is fascinating.

![A runner on a trail during a long run](/blog/assets/what-happens-to-your-body-during-a-long-run/image_1.jpg)

As the miles add up, your body shifts through several distinct physiological phases. Your cardiovascular system ramps up cardiac output, your muscles burn through glycogen stores, and your core temperature rises as most of that energy converts to heat. Blood flow gets redirected toward working muscles, and your digestive system takes a back seat, which is why GI discomfort is so common on longer efforts.

The good news is that these stresses are also what drive adaptation. With consistent training, your heart becomes more efficient, your muscles develop greater mitochondrial density, and your body learns to tap fat as a fuel source more effectively.

---

## The First Few Minutes: Your Body Shifts Into Gear

The moment you anticipate a hard effort, your adrenal glands release adrenaline, raising your heart rate and redirecting blood toward working muscles. Within the first few minutes, cardiac output climbs rapidly and breathing deepens to pull in more oxygen.

This is also when pacing discipline matters most. Going out too hard depletes glycogen early and sets you up for a rough final miles.

### What Happens to Your Cardiovascular System

Your heart rate rises steadily as effort increases. One important phenomenon to understand is **cardiac drift**: as a run extends past an hour, your heart rate can creep upward by 5 to 20 beats per minute even if your pace stays the same. This is often a signal of dehydration or overheating, and it represents added strain that compounds over time.

Your respiratory rate also increases quickly to clear carbon dioxide and deliver more oxygen. Trained runners handle this more efficiently because consistent long runs improve breathing mechanics and raise VO2 max over time.

## Energy Systems and Fuel: How Your Body Powers Through

![A runner taking an energy gel during a long training run, illustrating mid-run fueling strategy](/blog/assets/what-happens-to-your-body-during-a-long-run/image_2.jpg)

Glycogen, stored in your muscles and liver, is the primary fuel source during a long run. Without fueling, most runners begin to deplete glycogen significantly after 60 to 90 minutes of sustained effort. When stores run critically low, you hit what runners call "the wall," a dramatic slowdown caused by the body scrambling to shift fuel sources.

To avoid this, start taking in simple carbohydrates around the 60 to 90 minute mark. Energy gels, chews, or sports drinks help maintain blood glucose and keep you moving.

### Hydration and Heat

Most of the energy your body produces during a run is released as heat. To regulate core temperature, you sweat, and fluid loss adds up fast. A practical guideline is to aim for roughly 15 to 30 ounces of fluid per hour.

Dehydration as small as 2 percent of body weight can meaningfully reduce performance. Beyond fluid loss, you also lose electrolytes, particularly sodium and potassium, which are critical for muscle contraction and fluid balance. Sports drinks or electrolyte supplements help replace both during longer efforts.

### How Your Muscles Work and Break Down

Long runs primarily recruit slow-twitch muscle fibers, built for endurance and fatigue resistance. Over the course of a long run, repetitive loading causes micro-tears in muscle tissue. This is normal. The inflammatory response that follows triggers adaptive repair, gradually making those muscles stronger and more resilient.

## GI Distress and Connective Tissue Stress

One of the most common surprises during a long run is gastrointestinal discomfort. Blood flow to the gut can be reduced by up to 80 percent during intense running as the body prioritizes muscles. This slows digestion and can cause nausea, cramping, and acid reflux. The best approach is trial and error during training, identifying which foods and fueling strategies your gut tolerates best before race day.

![A runner stretching their legs and lower body post-run, highlighting muscle and connective tissue recovery](/blog/assets/what-happens-to-your-body-during-a-long-run/image_3.jpg)

Running also loads your bones, tendons, and ligaments with every stride. Over time, this stress stimulates bone remodeling and can improve lower-body bone density. However, too much load too soon is how stress fractures and shin splints develop. Tendons adapt more slowly than muscles, which is why the widely cited 10 percent mileage rule exists: avoid increasing weekly mileage by more than 10 percent to give connective tissue time to catch up.

## The Mental Side of Going Long

Long runs build mental resilience in ways that shorter efforts cannot fully replicate. Sustaining effort through discomfort and fatigue teaches your brain that you can keep going even when everything signals otherwise.

Strategies like breaking the run into smaller segments, focusing on form, or using music and podcasts help manage attention and reduce perceived effort. ## Recovery and Long-Term Adaptation

Normal post-run fatigue includes 1 to 2 days of muscle soreness and mild stiffness. Warning signs of overtraining include soreness lasting more than 3 days, gait changes, and a resting heart rate that stays elevated for more than 24 hours.

Post-run nutrition matters significantly. Consuming protein and carbohydrates within the recovery window helps repair micro-tears and replenish glycogen. Sleep is when the most meaningful adaptation occurs.

With consistent training, the adaptations compound. Your heart's stroke volume increases, your capillary network expands, and mitochondria grow in both size and number. Your body also becomes better at [carbohydrate sparing](https://www.runnersworld.com/training/a34789560/long-run/), using fat as a fuel source more readily and preserving glycogen for harder efforts. Regular running reduces all-cause mortality by approximately 27 percent and lowers cardiovascular disease risk, making the long run one of the highest-return investments in your overall health.

## How to Know If Your Long Run Is Working

Distinguishing productive fatigue from excessive fatigue is a skill worth developing. Normal signs include temporary soreness and slightly sluggish early miles in the next session that clear up within 20 minutes. Concerning signs include pain that changes your gait, soreness that lingers beyond 3 days, or chronic low energy that affects daily function.

Monitor your resting heart rate in the 24 hours after a long run. An elevated reading that persists is a reliable signal that your body needs more recovery time. Track your training load over weeks, not just days, and respect the 10 percent weekly increase guideline. If fatigue feels abnormal or persistent, reduce volume, prioritize sleep, and seek guidance before a small problem becomes an injury.
