# A Realistic Guide to Marathon Training for Normal People

If you have a marathon on the calendar and a life full of work, family, and everything else in between, this is **a realistic guide to marathon training for normal people**. No elite coaching required, no six-day-a-week commitment, and no need to obsess over every split. Just a sensible, honest framework for getting to that finish line.

![A runner training on a road at an easy, conversational pace](/blog/assets/a-realistic-guide-to-marathon-training-for-normal-people/image_1.jpg)

Most marathon runners are regular people squeezing runs in before work, during lunch breaks, or on weekends. The key is building a consistent habit over 16 to 20 weeks, respecting recovery, and showing up regularly even when motivation dips. At Trekon, we believe running should fit your life, not replace it.

## Are You Ready to Start Training? Honest Prerequisites

Before jumping into a plan, check your current fitness honestly.

- Can you run 3 to 4 miles without stopping?
- Are you logging any consistent weekly mileage?
- Do you have any injury history that needs attention?

If your weekly mileage is below 15 to 20 miles, spend 4 to 6 weeks in a base-building phase first. It is the foundation that keeps you healthy through the harder weeks ahead.

## Choosing the Right Plan

Most training plans run 16 to 20 weeks. A 16-week plan suits runners with a solid base. A 20-week plan gives beginners more breathing room. [Hal Higdon's marathon programs](https://www.halhigdon.com) offer novice through advanced options and are a great free starting point. Whatever plan you choose, treat it as an outline, not a rigid contract. Life happens, and flexibility is part of the process.

## Understanding the Four Training Phases

### Phase 1 - Base Building (Weeks 1-4)

Build the habit of running consistently. Keep every run at an easy, conversational pace. If you cannot hold a sentence, slow down. Your long run starts short, perhaps 5 to 7 miles, and grows gradually.

### Phase 2 - Building Volume (Weeks 5-8)

Follow the 10% rule: never increase your total weekly mileage by more than 10% from one week to the next. This single habit prevents the majority of overuse injuries. Introduce one short tempo run per week at a comfortably hard effort.

### Phase 3 - Peak Training (Weeks 9-16)

![A runner on a long training run through a park, showing fatigue and determination mid-effort](/blog/assets/a-realistic-guide-to-marathon-training-for-normal-people/image_2.jpg)

This is the hardest block. Include race-pace segments within some long runs to teach your body what marathon effort feels like. Your long run peaks at 16 to 18 miles. You do not need to run 20-plus miles in training. Research consistently shows that long runs beyond two and a half hours produce diminishing returns and increase injury risk. Include a tune-up race, such as a half marathon, to simulate race conditions.

### Phase 4 - Taper (Weeks 17-18)

Volume drops significantly, but intensity stays. Shorter runs with some race-pace effort keep your legs sharp without adding fatigue. Prioritise sleep and increase carbohydrate intake in the final days. Trust your training.

## How to Structure Your Training Week

Four runs per week is the optimal frequency for most recreational marathoners. Here is a simple weekly framework:

| Day | Session |
| --- | --- |
| Monday | Rest or cross-training |
| Tuesday | Easy run |
| Wednesday | Speed or tempo session |
| Thursday | Easy run |
| Friday | Rest |
| Saturday | Long run |
| Sunday | Rest or light cross-training |

Easy runs should be 60 to 90 seconds per mile slower than your goal marathon pace. Rest days are not optional extras. They are where adaptation happens.

## The Long Run: Your Most Important Workout

Time on feet matters more than pace on long runs. Use the talk test: if you can speak in full sentences, you are at the right effort. Build distance gradually each week, with a cutback week every third or fourth week. Cap your longest run at 16 to 18 miles. Running on tired legs during these sessions prepares you for the final miles of the race.

## Pacing: Why Slowing Down Makes You Faster

Most recreational runners train too fast on easy days and pay for it with fatigue and injury. Easy effort means Zone 2 heart rate, roughly an RPE of 3 to 4 out of 10. Your goal marathon pace should feel sustainable. In the race itself, start the first half 30 to 60 seconds per mile slower than goal pace. The second half is where races are won or lost.

## Nutrition and Fueling

![A runner taking an energy gel during a long run with a water bottle in hand](/blog/assets/a-realistic-guide-to-marathon-training-for-normal-people/image_3.jpg)

Fueling is a skill you practice in training, not something you figure out on race day.

- **Daily carbohydrates:** Aim for 2.5 to 3.5 grams per pound of body weight during training.
- **Carb-loading:** In the final 3 days before the race, increase to 3.5 to 4.5 grams per kilogram of body weight.
- **Race-day fueling:** Take in 30 to 60 grams of carbohydrates per hour after the first 45 minutes, using gels or chews every 30 to 45 minutes.
- **Hydration:** Sip 4 to 6 ounces of fluid every 15 to 20 minutes. Pale yellow urine is your target indicator.

Practice your fueling strategy on every long run so your stomach is trained alongside your legs.

## Injury Prevention: Staying Healthy

Strength work two to three times per week, focusing on glutes, hips, and core, is one of the highest-return habits in marathon training. Replace your running shoes every 300 to 500 miles. If you feel sharp pain, rest for 2 to 3 days and cross-train with cycling or swimming. Sleep 7 to 9 hours per night. When your body signals something is wrong, adjust the plan rather than push through.

## Fitting Training Into a Busy Life

Three to four running days per week is the minimum effective dose for most people. Protect your long run and your one quality session as non-negotiables. Pack your shoes when you travel. A hotel treadmill or an unfamiliar city street counts as a training run. Missed workouts are not catastrophic. What matters is the overall pattern, not any single day.

## Race Day Strategy for First-Timers

Start slower than you think you need to. The adrenaline of race day will make early miles feel effortless, and that is exactly the trap. Use a pace calculator to set your splits before you arrive. Break the race into mental checkpoints at each mile marker rather than thinking about 26.2 miles as one enormous number. If you hit the wall, slow down, take a gel, and keep moving.

## Recovery After the Marathon

Allow 2 to 3 full weeks of recovery before returning to structured running. Gentle walking in the first few days keeps blood moving without stress. Reflect on what worked and what you would do differently. Most runners who finish one marathon are already thinking about the next one before the medal is around their neck.
