# Periodisation Explained: How to Structure a Training Year

**Periodisation Explained: How to Structure a Training Year** is the difference between athletes who peak when it matters and those who grind through the year only to plateau.

Rather than training randomly or chasing fitness all at once, periodisation organises your training into deliberate cycles, each with a clear purpose, so your body can absorb stress, recover, and adapt progressively across the season.

![Diagram showing a training year divided into base, build, peak, and recovery phases with volume and intensity curves](/blog/assets/periodisation-explained-how-to-structure-a-training-year/image_1.jpg)

The concept is rooted in Hans Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome, which describes how the body responds to stress through alarm, resistance, and - if pushed too far - exhaustion. A well-structured training year keeps you in that productive resistance phase, driving consistent gains while reducing overtraining and injury risk.

This guide breaks down the macro-, meso-, and microcycle framework, the key phases of a training year, and how to sequence them so your fitness peaks at exactly the right moment.

## What Is Periodisation and Why Does It Matter?

Periodisation is the deliberate organisation of training into planned cycles, each targeting a specific adaptation before the next phase begins. Without structure, training stress accumulates without direction, pushing athletes toward exhaustion rather than productive adaptation.

The concept was formalised in the 1960s by Soviet physiologist Leo Matveyev, who identified cycled loading as the key driver of long-term performance gains.

Core benefits of periodisation:

- Progressive overload applied systematically across the season
- Reduced injury and overtraining risk through built-in recovery blocks
- Fitness peaks timed to race day rather than mid-training block

## Periodisation Explained: How to Structure a Training Year Using Cycles

Training cycles nest inside each other. The macrocycle sets the annual blueprint, mesocycles carve it into focused phases, and microcycles translate those phases into weekly reality.

### The Macrocycle: Your Big-Picture Training Year

A macrocycle spans several months to a full year, anchored by one primary goal event - your A-race. Most runners complete one or two macrocycles per year, targeting a spring race and a fall race with a short transition phase bridging the two.

### The Mesocycle: Building Blocks of the Season

Mesocycles typically run 4-6 weeks, each with a single dominant focus: aerobic base, lactate threshold, or race-specific power. The standard structure follows a 3:1 ratio - three weeks of progressive load followed by one recovery week.

### The Microcycle: Weekly Training in Detail

The microcycle is your weekly schedule. A well-designed week places hard sessions where recovery is available, balances key workouts with easy days, and builds load progressively across the mesocycle.

A typical structured running week:

- Monday/Friday: rest or active recovery
- Tuesday: quality session (threshold or intervals)
- Wednesday/Thursday: easy aerobic runs
- Saturday: long run
- Sunday: easy recovery run

## The Four Phases of a Periodised Training Year

The four phases always follow the same sequence. Reversing or skipping phases reliably leads to plateau or injury.

### Base Phase (8-16 Weeks): Building Your Aerobic Foundation

The base phase emphasises high volume at low intensity, keeping most running in Zone 2. Physiological targets include mitochondrial density, fat oxidation capacity, and cardiac stroke volume. Supplementary strength training fits naturally here, building structural resilience before race-specific stress arrives.

### Build Phase (6-10 Weeks): Introducing Race-Specific Intensity

The build phase layers threshold runs, tempo intervals, and VO2max work onto the aerobic foundation. Total volume reduces slightly - typically 10-20% - to make room for quality. The goal is to raise the lactate threshold and improve race-pace efficiency, not to peak.

### Peak and Taper (2-4 Weeks): Sharpening for Race Day

Volume drops 40-60% during the taper while intensity is maintained to preserve neuromuscular sharpness. This sheds accumulated fatigue so the fitness built over months can finally surface on race day.

### Transition Phase (1-2 Weeks): Recovery and Reset

The transition phase is unstructured by design. Removing scheduled sessions allows the nervous system, tendons, and connective tissue to recover fully. Active rest such as walking or easy cycling maintains movement without imposing training stress.

## How Periodisation Explained: How to Structure a Training Year Applies to Runners

A 5K or 10K plan may compress into 16-20 weeks; a marathon build typically spans 24-32 weeks. Two macrocycles per year is the most common structure for runners targeting both a spring and a fall season.

Key running-specific applications:

- Insert cutback weeks every third or fourth week to prevent burnout
- Treat B-races as hard training days within a mesocycle, not secondary peaks
- Anchor every plan to the A-race date and reverse-engineer all phases from it

According to [Periodization: Planned Peaks and Targeted Training](https://sirc.ca/articles/periodization-planned-peaks-and-targeted-training/), staying in the resistance phase of the General Adaptation Syndrome is the central goal of any sound periodisation plan.

## Linear vs Block Periodisation: Which Model Should You Use?

The traditional linear model progressively increases intensity while decreasing volume across the macrocycle. Changes are gradual and predictable, making it the most accessible entry point for newer athletes.

Block periodisation concentrates a single training stimulus into 7-14 day doses before switching focus, generating sharp adaptations in targeted systems. Undulating periodisation varies load within the week itself, suiting athletes who need frequent stimulus changes.

A simple model selection guide:

- Beginners: linear periodisation, one phase focus at a time
- Intermediate: traditional linear with structured mesocycles and planned recovery weeks
- Advanced: block or undulating periodisation, higher variation, tighter stimulus windows

## How to Build Your Own Annual Training Plan

Start by identifying your A-race date and working backward. Assign taper (2-4 weeks), then build (6-10 weeks), then base (8-16 weeks), checking that the calendar can accommodate each block at minimum length before committing.

Steps to reverse-engineer your plan:

- Set your A-race date and count weeks backward through taper, build, and base
- Schedule a recovery week every third or fourth week within each phase
- Mark B-races and adjust surrounding microcycles to treat them as hard training days
- Review the full plan monthly and adjust for illness, travel, or unexpected load

## Common Periodisation Mistakes to Avoid

![Annotated annual training calendar highlighting a compressed base phase, consecutive A-races with no transition, and absent recovery weeks](/blog/assets/periodisation-explained-how-to-structure-a-training-year/image_3.jpg)

Most periodisation errors share a common theme: impatience. Athletes rush the base phase, schedule too many A-races, or skip recovery weeks, then wonder why fitness plateaus or injuries appear mid-season.

Mistakes to avoid:

- Skipping the base phase - without an aerobic foundation, intensity work hits a hard ceiling no intervals can raise
- Peaking too early - compressing base to 4-5 weeks causes form to peak in spring and flatline by summer
- Too many A-races - three per year is the practical maximum; more prevents proper rebuilding between cycles
- Ignoring recovery weeks - the body adapts during rest, not during load; skipping the 3:1 ratio drives overreaching
- Using one-size-fits-all plans - phase lengths and volume targets must match individual fitness history and goals

## Frequently Asked Questions About Periodisation

**What is a macrocycle?**
A macrocycle is the largest training unit - typically a full season or year - encompassing all phases from base through transition and anchored to a goal competition.

**How long should the base phase be for a beginner?**
An 8-12 week base is the practical minimum for most beginners. A longer base raises the ceiling for all subsequent intensity work and reduces injury risk in the build phase.

**Can beginners periodise?**
Yes. A simplified base-build-taper structure is effective from the earliest stages of training and requires no complex block or undulating models to produce results.

**How many phases can I fit in a year?**
Most runners complete one to four macrocycles annually. Two is the most practical number for athletes targeting both spring and fall race seasons.
