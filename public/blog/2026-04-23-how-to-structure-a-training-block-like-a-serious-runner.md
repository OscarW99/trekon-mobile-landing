# How to Structure a Training Block Like a Serious Runner

Most runners train consistently but not intentionally. They log miles week after week with no clear progression, then wonder why race day feels harder than expected. Learning **how to structure a training block like a serious runner** changes that entirely, replacing guesswork with a deliberate system that builds fitness in the right order, at the right time.

![Runner following a structured training block on a trail](/blog/assets/how-to-structure-a-training-block-like-a-serious-runner/image_1.jpg)

Elite athletes don't just run a lot. They organize their training into focused cycles that balance hard efforts, targeted workouts, and planned recovery. That same logic, known as periodization, separates runners who peak on race day from those who arrive flat or injured. Whether you're chasing a marathon PR or finishing your first half, this approach gives every run a purpose and every week a role in a larger plan.

## What Most Runners Get Wrong About Training

Most recreational runners fall into the same trap: they run similar distances at similar paces week after week, with no clear destination in mind. Without a goal race anchoring the plan, there are no phases, no progression, and no reason for any single run to exist beyond habit.

The result is predictable. Mileage stays flat, fitness plateaus, and the body either breaks down from repetitive stress or stagnates from lack of stimulus. Structure fixes this by replacing hope with a deliberate system.

## What Is a Training Block (and Why It Matters)

A training block is a defined period of training organized around a specific goal race, built on periodization: the science of organizing stress and recovery into progressive cycles.

Tudor Bompa, who formalized modern periodization theory in the 1960s, described three levels of structure:

- **Macrocycle**: the full training season, typically 12 to 24 weeks
- **Mesocycle**: a focused block of several weeks targeting a specific adaptation
- **Microcycle**: the individual week, with its mix of hard, easy, and recovery days

The goal is to arrive at your target race at peak fitness, not just adequately prepared.

## The Four Core Phases of a Training Block

A well-built training block moves through four phases in a specific order: Base, Build, Peak, and Recovery. The sequence is not arbitrary. Each phase creates the conditions the next one requires. Volume before intensity is the golden rule. You build the aerobic engine first, then add harder work on top of it.

### Phase 1: Base Building (8-12+ Weeks)

The base phase is the least glamorous and the most important. Its purpose is to develop your aerobic foundation before any serious intensity enters the picture.

Key elements of a strong base phase:

- Run at a conversational, Zone 2 pace for the majority of your mileage
- Increase weekly volume gradually, no more than 10% per week
- Prioritize frequency over distance
- Add strides once or twice a week for neuromuscular activation
- Include consistent strength work targeting glutes, hips, and core

There are no intervals in this phase. The work feels easy because it is supposed to, building the capillary density, mitochondrial efficiency, and connective tissue resilience that supports everything that comes later.

### Phase 2: Build Phase (6-8 Weeks)

Once the aerobic base is established, the build phase introduces controlled intensity. One quality session per week is enough to drive adaptation without overwhelming recovery.

Typical build phase workouts include tempo runs at lactate threshold pace, hill repeats for running-specific strength, and moderate long runs with gradual weekly progression. You should finish workouts feeling challenged but not destroyed. A tune-up race in this phase is a useful fitness check and a chance to practice race-day logistics.

### Phase 3: Peak Phase (2-4 Weeks)

![Runner doing race-pace interval training on a road at dawn](/blog/assets/how-to-structure-a-training-block-like-a-serious-runner/image_2.jpg)

The peak phase sharpens the fitness you have built. Weekly mileage drops, long runs get shorter and faster, and quality sessions become more race-specific.

Renato Canova, who has coached more athletes to sub-2:05 marathons than any other coach in history, structures peak work around 90 to 110 percent of marathon pace. During this phase, recovery becomes as important as the workouts themselves. Sleep, nutrition, and stress management are the mechanism through which fitness is absorbed and expressed.

### Phase 4: Taper and Recovery (1-3 Weeks Post-Race)

The taper is the final reduction before race day. Volume drops by roughly 20 to 50 percent over one to three weeks, but intensity is maintained. Cutting pace during the taper is a common mistake that leaves runners feeling flat at the start line.

After the race, the recovery phase begins. Light activity, mobility work, and honest reflection on what worked set up the next cycle. Most serious runners complete one to two full cycles per year.

## How the Weekly Structure Fits Inside a Training Block

Within each phase, the weekly structure follows a hard-easy pattern. Hard days are followed by easy or rest days to allow adaptation. The long run typically anchors the weekend, with one midweek quality session during the build and peak phases.

A cutback week every three to four weeks reduces volume by 20 to 30 percent to let accumulated fatigue dissipate. A sample base week might look like this:

| Day | Session |
| --- | --- |
| Monday | Rest or easy 30 min |
| Tuesday | Easy 45-60 min with strides |
| Wednesday | Easy 45 min |
| Thursday | Easy 60 min |
| Friday | Rest or easy 30 min |
| Saturday | Long run, easy pace |
| Sunday | Easy 30-40 min recovery |

## What Elite Training Blocks Look Like (And What to Borrow)

![Elite marathon runner training on an empty road with a coach on a bicycle alongside](/blog/assets/how-to-structure-a-training-block-like-a-serious-runner/image_3.jpg)

Sebastian Sawe, the defending London Marathon champion, trains on a 10-day cycle rather than a standard seven-day week. His structure includes long runs progressing from 25 km on gravel to 40 km on asphalt, double days with morning and afternoon sessions, fartlek and interval work, dedicated hill repeats, and recovery runs offsetting every hard effort.

You can [learn from Sebastian Sawe's 10-day training cycle](https://www.runnersworld.com/uk/training/marathon/a71027488/sebastian-sawe-training-cycle/), but treat it as a reference point, not a template. The principles translate: mix quality with volume, balance hard days with recovery, and use hill work and intervals to build strength and speed. The exact distances and paces do not.

## How to Structure a Training Block Around Your Life

Start with your goal race on the calendar and work backwards. Reserve 8 to 10 weeks for race-specific work and taper, then bridge the gap with base building after your previous recovery period.

Plan around real life. Holidays, travel, and work demands are predictable, so build them into the structure rather than letting them derail it. A missed week during base phase is far less costly than a missed week during peak phase.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Increasing volume and intensity at the same time, which multiplies injury risk
- Skipping the base phase and jumping straight to intervals
- Omitting recovery weeks, leading to accumulated fatigue and overtraining
- Ignoring strength work, which leaves connective tissue vulnerable as mileage rises

For a deeper look at [periodization principles for runners](https://www.runnersworld.com/advanced/a42218246/periodization-training-guide/), the science behind phase sequencing is worth understanding before you build your first block.

## Key Takeaways

- Move through Base, Build, Peak, and Recovery in that order, every time
- Volume before intensity is the rule that prevents most training injuries
- Anchor everything to a specific goal race and work backwards from that date
- Consistency over heroics produces the long-term gains that race-day performances are built on
- Repeat the cycle one to two times per year, and each block will start from a higher fitness floor than the last
