# Running During Pregnancy: What's Safe, What's Not, and What Changes

For most healthy pregnancies, lacing up your shoes is not only permitted but actively encouraged by major medical organizations like the [American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/exercise-during-pregnancy).

![Pregnant woman running outdoors on a flat path, wearing supportive athletic gear](/blog/assets/running-during-pregnancy-whats-safe-whats-not-and-what-changes/image_1.jpg)

That said, pregnancy transforms your body in ways that directly affect how you run and when you may need to stop. Your center of gravity shifts, joints loosen under relaxin, and your cardiovascular system works harder even at a comfortable pace. This guide breaks down what current research and clinical guidance say, trimester by trimester, so you can keep moving safely.

## How Pregnancy Transforms Your Running Physiology

Pregnancy triggers physical changes that affect every run. Understanding them helps you adapt rather than fight your body.

- **Relaxin hormone**: Loosens ligaments and joints, increasing injury risk in the hips, pelvis, and knees.
- **Center of gravity**: Your bump shifts balance forward, altering gait and increasing fall risk.
- **Cardiovascular load**: Blood volume increases by up to 50 percent, so your heart works harder even at easy paces. Pre-pregnancy pace benchmarks become unreliable.
- **Overheating risk**: Core temperature rises during pregnancy. Sustained temperatures above 102°F in the first trimester carry theoretical risks, making heat management essential.
- **Breathing capacity**: The growing uterus pushes against the diaphragm, causing breathlessness at efforts that once felt comfortable.

Regular running during pregnancy is linked to reduced gestational diabetes risk, lower blood pressure, better weight management, shorter labor, improved mood, and potential benefits to baby brain development, according to research cited by [Runner's World](https://www.runnersworld.com/training/a20849722/what-you-need-to-know-about-running-and-pregnancy/).

## Is It Safe to Run While Pregnant?

If you were running regularly before pregnancy and your pregnancy is uncomplicated, you can almost certainly keep going. ACOG recommends at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week for most pregnant people, and running counts.

**Who should not run:** Stop and consult your provider if you have vaginal bleeding, placenta previa, preeclampsia, a multiple pregnancy with preterm labor risk, or significant heart or lung disease. Beginners should start with brisk walking, not running.

**Warning signs to stop immediately:** heavy bleeding, chest pain, dizziness, severe uterine contractions, sudden muscle weakness, calf swelling, or extreme breathlessness at rest.

## What Changes Trimester by Trimester

### First Trimester

![Pregnant woman in early pregnancy jogging on a treadmill indoors in a well-lit gym](/blog/assets/running-during-pregnancy-whats-safe-whats-not-and-what-changes/image_2.jpg)

The first trimester is often unpredictable. Fatigue and nausea can arrive without warning.

- Reduce intensity on bad days. A short easy jog beats skipping, but rest is also valid.
- Avoid running in heat or humidity. Run in the early morning or move indoors.
- Hydrate well before, during, and after every run.
- Slow down, shorten distances, and listen to your body above all else.

### Second Trimester

Many runners find the second trimester the most manageable. Nausea eases, energy returns, and the bump is present but not yet limiting.

- Stay aware of your footing as your balance shifts.
- Introduce walk breaks and stick to flat, even surfaces.
- Use the **talk test**: if you cannot hold a conversation, you are working too hard.
- Drop performance goals. This trimester is about maintaining fitness, not setting personal bests.

### Third Trimester

The third trimester brings the most significant changes, and running may become uncomfortable for many women.

- Lower back pain and pelvic girdle pain are common and can make running painful.
- Fall risk increases significantly on uneven terrain.
- Walking, swimming, and stationary cycling are excellent alternatives that maintain fitness without the impact.
- Some women run comfortably to their due date; others switch at 28 weeks. Both are valid.

## Practical Adjustments for Your Training Plan

Adapting your training is essential from the moment you get a positive test.

- **Scale back mileage**: Reduce distance significantly and adjust based on how you feel each day.
- **Surface choice**: Treadmills and flat paved paths are safer than trails or uneven terrain.
- **Footwear**: Feet can swell and widen during pregnancy. Get properly fitted shoes with good support.
- **Avoid heat**: Skip outdoor runs on hot, humid days. Saunas and hot tubs are off-limits throughout pregnancy.

**Monitoring intensity:** Your usual pace targets are no longer reliable. Use the talk test, aim for a 4 to 6 on a 1 to 10 RPE scale, and run by feel rather than the clock.

A good coaching app can adjust your plan based on trimester, energy levels, and symptoms. [Trekon](https://www.trekon.run) offers personalised running plans that adapt as your pregnancy progresses, helping you stay active safely.

## Returning to Running After Pregnancy

Recovery takes longer than most new mothers expect, and the pelvic floor needs dedicated rehabilitation before running resumes.

![Postpartum woman doing gentle stretching exercises on a yoga mat in a bright living room](/blog/assets/running-during-pregnancy-whats-safe-whats-not-and-what-changes/image_3.jpg)

Most guidelines suggest waiting at least six weeks postpartum before returning to running, and longer after a cesarean or complicated delivery. Walk before you run, rebuild your aerobic base gradually, and prioritise pelvic floor rehabilitation with a women's health physiotherapist if possible. A gradual return protects against long-term issues like prolapse and incontinence.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Will Running Cause a Miscarriage or Harm the Baby?

No. Research consistently shows no evidence that running causes miscarriage or harms the baby in uncomplicated pregnancies. The baby is well protected by the uterus and amniotic fluid. The key qualifier is always an uncomplicated pregnancy with medical clearance.

### Can I Start Running for the First Time While Pregnant?

This is not recommended. Starting a running program during pregnancy puts untrained joints and ligaments under load at the worst possible time. Begin with brisk walking, stationary cycling, or swimming instead.

### How Do I Know When to Stop Running During Pregnancy?

Your body will usually tell you. Common signals include persistent pelvic pain, breathlessness at easy paces, or a general sense that running no longer feels right. In the later weeks of the third trimester, many women naturally transition to walking without a specific trigger. Trust that instinct - maintaining fitness through low-impact movement is just as valuable as running.
