# What Running Has in Common With Meditation (More Than You Think)

At first glance, running and meditation seem like opposites. One demands movement, sweat, and effort; the other asks for stillness and quiet. But **what running has in common with meditation (more than you think)** goes far deeper than most people realize, touching the same brain networks, the same stress hormones, and the same core mental skills.

![Runner on a trail in a calm, focused state, illustrating the meditative qualities of running](/blog/assets/what-running-has-in-common-with-meditation-more-than-you-think/image_1.jpg)

Research using functional MRI scans shows that both practices quiet the brain's default mode network while boosting activity in areas tied to attention and mood. Both also trigger the release of endorphins, endocannabinoids, and serotonin, creating that calm, clear-headed feeling you recognize after a good run or a solid meditation session.

Understanding this connection can genuinely change how you approach your training, opening the door to running that feels less like a grind and more like a moving practice that builds mental resilience alongside physical fitness.

## Why Runners and Meditators Are After the Same Thing

On the surface, a meditator sitting in silence and a runner pounding a trail seem to want completely different things. But dig a little deeper and the shared goal becomes clear: both are chasing mental clarity, a quieter mind, and a stronger sense of presence.

Both practices also demand repetition and discipline. You do not become a meditator after one session, and you do not become a runner after one kilometre. The benefits accumulate through consistent return, day after day. Buddhist monks have used walking and running as contemplative practices for centuries, and today neuroscience is confirming what practitioners always knew: the two paths lead to the same inner territory.

## The Core Similarities

### Both Practices Use the Breath as an Anchor

In seated meditation, the breath is the classic anchor. Running works the same way. Your breathing rhythm becomes the metronome that keeps attention grounded in the present moment. When pace and breath fall into sync, the mind naturally narrows its focus, and when attention drifts, the breath is always there to bring you back.

### Both Quiet the Brain's Default Mode Network

The default mode network (DMN) is most active when you are doing nothing in particular. It generates self-referential thought, mental chatter, and rumination. Both running and meditation reduce DMN activity, producing that calm alertness where thoughts still exist but lose their grip. This is not a metaphor; it is measurable neuroscience, as detailed by [Marathon Handbook on brain states during running](https://marathonhandbook.com/your-brain-on-a-run-looks-a-lot-like-your-brain-in-meditation/).

### Both Build Present-Moment Awareness Over Time

A randomised controlled trial (N=413) published in the Journal of Health Psychology compared mindfulness-based stress reduction and moderate aerobic exercise over eight weeks. Both improved mental health through remarkably similar mechanisms. The key mediating factor was mindfulness self-efficacy: the belief in one's ability to stay present under pressure. Regular runners develop this quality even without formal meditation practice.

As [Runnerona on the science of running and meditation](https://www.runnerona.com/when-running-becomes-meditation-the-science-of-two-ancient-practices/) puts it: the body can be a path to teaching the mind.

### Both Involve Confronting Discomfort With Equanimity

Long meditation sessions surface restless thoughts and emotional tension. Long runs surface physical exhaustion and the urge to quit. In both cases, the practice is not to eliminate the discomfort but to sit with it without being overwhelmed. Consistent practice in either domain builds resilience that carries into the other.

## What Happens Inside Your Brain

### Shared Neurochemistry

A study published in Biological Psychology examined beta-endorphin, cortisol, and corticotropin-releasing hormone levels in both runners and meditators. Both groups showed mood improvements linked to overlapping hormonal shifts. Running and meditation also both raise endocannabinoid and serotonin levels. The calm feeling you recognise after a good run is biochemically close to what a meditator experiences after a solid session.

### Flow States: Where Running and Meditation Converge

![Close-up of running shoes hitting a trail surface mid-stride, symbolising grounded attention and body awareness](/blog/assets/what-running-has-in-common-with-meditation-more-than-you-think/image_2.jpg)

Flow is a state of calm, absorbed focus where performance feels effortless and time distorts. Both running and meditation can produce it, and both require operating at the edge of your comfort zone: engaged enough to stay present, relaxed enough not to force it. The zone that runners describe and the spaciousness that meditators report are different words for the same neurological event.

## How Mindful Running Puts Both Practices Together

Mindful running is not slow running or spiritual running. It is intentional running: choosing to notice rather than drift. That means attending to foot strike, breath rhythm, body sensations, and environmental cues rather than letting the mind run on autopilot.

**Practical steps to try on your next run:**

- Start slow and build pace gradually, letting awareness settle before intensity rises.
- Breathe through the nose where possible; relax the exhale.
- Leave the earbuds behind for at least part of the run.
- Check in with the body every few minutes: feet, hips, shoulders, jaw.
- When the mind wanders, return to breath or foot strike without criticism.

Before you start, choose a simple guiding word or present-focused statement such as "easy and steady." This functions as a mental anchor and shifts a workout from mechanical to purposeful. ## Real-World Proof

### The Monk Who Runs Every Morning

Yukai Shimizu is a Zen Buddhist monk based in Nagano, Japan, who runs mountain trails every morning before his temple duties begin. For Yukai, the two practices are inseparable. "For me as a monk, to run is to live in the moment," he has said. Running gives him space to work through self-doubt with compassion rather than avoidance. It is devotion in motion.

### What Consistent Runners Already Know

Most experienced runners can describe the post-run quiet without being able to explain it. The inner critic softens. Creative thoughts arrive uninvited. Problems that felt urgent before the run seem smaller after it. These are not side effects of exercise; they are the same outcomes meditators work toward deliberately.

## How to Deepen the Connection

### Signs Your Run Is Already Meditative

- Time distorts: forty minutes feels like fifteen.
- Worry reduces noticeably during and after the run.
- You feel body gratitude rather than body criticism when you finish.
- The habit loop feels self-reinforcing; you return because it genuinely helps.

### When to Combine Formal Meditation With Your Training

![Runner pausing on a hilltop at sunrise, hands relaxed, gazing at the landscape, illustrating the meditative afterglow of a mindful run](/blog/assets/what-running-has-in-common-with-meditation-more-than-you-think/image_3.jpg)

- Sit for five to ten minutes before a run to settle the nervous system.
- Use rest days for longer meditation sessions, treating them as mental cross-training.
- After a hard race or workout, a short body-scan meditation deepens recovery.

Over time, a dual practice produces calmer racing, steadier pacing decisions, and more patience with the process.

## The Takeaway: Movement and Stillness Are Two Doors to the Same Room

Running and meditation are not competing practices. They are complementary tools that work through overlapping brain networks, shared neurochemistry, and the same core skill: returning attention to the present moment, again and again, without judgement.

The repetition is the point. Each run is a session. Each breath is a reset. Whether you come to this connection through curiosity or a desire to get more from your training, the invitation is the same: next time you lace up, treat the run as a practice rather than just a workout. The mental and physical gains are not separate. They never were.
