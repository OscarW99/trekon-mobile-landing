# Vitamin D and Winter Running Performance

Every winter, millions of runners log miles in cold, low-light conditions without realizing their body may be running short on one critical nutrient.

**Vitamin D and winter running performance** are more closely linked than many athletes expect, because the sun's UVB rays, which trigger around 80% of the body's vitamin D production, are too weak at northern latitudes to maintain adequate levels for months at a time.

![Runner training outdoors in winter sunlight with snow on the ground, illustrating the challenge of maintaining vitamin D levels during cold-weather training](/blog/assets/vitamin-d-and-winter-running-performance/image_1.jpg)

Research confirms the concern is real: studies show that 56% of athletes have inadequate vitamin D levels, with risk peaking in winter and spring. Low levels connect to illness, stress fractures, muscle weakness, and slower recovery - all things that quietly erode a training block.

The picture gets more nuanced when you look at what supplementation actually does. Raising vitamin D levels through supplementation or outdoor exposure helps stabilize immune markers and may reduce muscle damage after hard efforts, but the evidence for direct performance gains in VO2max or explosive power is mixed.

What matters most is understanding your status and addressing a genuine deficiency before it costs you training time.

## Why Winter Changes Everything for Runners

UVB radiation drops sharply above 35 degrees north from October to March. At UK and northern European latitudes, skin-based vitamin D synthesis is effectively zero from November through February, regardless of how much time a runner spends outside.

Dietary sources cannot bridge the gap alone:
- Food contributes only 10-20% of daily vitamin D needs even under ideal conditions
- Oily fish, the richest food source, delivers roughly 40-150 IU per serving
- Meeting a 2000 IU daily target through food alone is not realistic for most runners

A pooled analysis of 23 studies covering 2,313 athletes found 56% had inadequate vitamin D levels, with risk peaking in winter, spring, and at higher latitudes.

## What Vitamin D Actually Does for Runners

![Runner stretching on a snow-covered trail at dawn, illustrating how low winter light affects vitamin D synthesis during cold-weather training sessions](/blog/assets/vitamin-d-and-winter-running-performance/image_2.jpg)

Vitamin D modulates more than 2,000 genes tied to cellular growth, immune function, and protein synthesis. For runners, three areas carry the most practical weight.

### Bone Health and Stress Fracture Risk

Vitamin D governs calcium absorption and bone turnover rate. Runners log thousands of high-impact strides per session, making bone quality a direct performance variable.

- Low vitamin D links to elevated bone turnover markers and reduced bone mineral density
- Stress fracture rates are higher in athletes with deficient status
- Winter marathon training blocks create peak fracture risk when vitamin D is simultaneously lowest

### Muscle Function, Recovery, and Inflammation

Vitamin D receptors sit on fast-twitch muscle fibres. A three-week supplementation trial in endurance runners found significantly lower post-exercise troponin, myoglobin, and creatine kinase in the supplemented group versus placebo, alongside reduced TNF-alpha.

That combination - less muscle damage and a quieter inflammatory response - matters most after the eccentric loading of hills and long runs.

### Immune Resilience Through the Winter Months

An 8-week autumn-winter trial found that supplemented participants maintained more stable leukocyte and neutrophil counts across the season. Fewer immune dips translate directly into fewer training interruptions - often the most underrated benefit for a runner building a base.

## What the Research Shows

The research is consistent: supplementation corrects a deficiency floor but does not push performance above normal.

### Does Supplementation Improve VO2max?

In the [Gervasi et al. study published in Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-38528-1), 45 recreational runners received either 2000 IU/day vitamin D3 or placebo for 8 weeks from October to December. VO2max showed no significant change. A network meta-analysis of multiple trials confirmed the same finding: no ergogenic effect on aerobic capacity.

### Strength, Power, and Sprint Gains

The picture shifts when baseline vitamin D is genuinely low. In a UK winter study, 62% of professional athletes had 25(OH)D below 50 nmol/L. Those given 5000 IU/day for 8 weeks saw 10 m sprint times and vertical jump improve significantly (P = 0.008), while the placebo group showed no change.

A systematic review found 5 of 7 studies reported anaerobic power improvements with supplementation - but 4 of 6 found no sprint benefit. How deficient the athlete was at baseline appears to drive outcomes more than the supplement itself.
A military cohort study found each 1 nmol/L higher 25(OH)D corresponded to roughly 0.42-0.57 seconds faster on a 1.5-mile run. Yet when supplementation raised recruits to sufficiency, run times did not improve. Correcting a deficiency plausibly helps. Topping up beyond it does not.

## How Much Vitamin D Do Runners Need in Winter?

### Signs of Deficiency to Watch For

These symptoms overlap heavily with overtraining, which makes them easy to dismiss without a blood test:

- Persistent leg heaviness or muscle weakness that does not resolve with rest
- Unusual fatigue and low mood, particularly from November through February
- A cluster of minor illnesses that keeps disrupting training weeks
- Bone tenderness around load-bearing sites such as the tibia or metatarsals

### Testing, Target Levels, and Safe Dosing

A 25(OH)D blood test is the standard check, with most sports practitioners targeting 75-100 nmol/L for athletes in heavy training. The NHS recommends 10 mcg (400 IU) daily in autumn and winter for the general population.

Sports dietitians often use 2000-5000 IU for confirmed deficiency. Because vitamin D is fat-soluble and accumulates in tissue, high doses without monitoring carry toxicity risk - clinician input before sustained use of 5000 IU/day is worth seeking.

## Outdoor Running vs. Supplements

Research from the EHU-University of the Basque Country found that outdoor-training runners consistently showed better vitamin D status than supplement-taking non-runners. Even 20-30 minutes of easy running at solar noon in autumn captures meaningful UVB before the winter production window closes.

From November onwards at northern latitudes, supplements take over as the main maintenance tool. Their role is status protection, not performance enhancement.

## Practical Winter Strategies for Runners

![Runner preparing a post-workout meal with salmon and eggs, illustrating dietary vitamin D sources that support winter training recovery](/blog/assets/vitamin-d-and-winter-running-performance/image_3.jpg)

A few targeted steps reduce winter deficiency risk without over-engineering nutrition:

- **Diet first**: oily fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines), egg yolks, and fortified milks or cereals form a useful base layer
- **Start early**: begin supplementation in October, not December, to build stores before the deepest UVB deficit arrives
- **Dose sensibly**: 2000 IU/day maintains levels for most runners; confirmed deficiency may warrant 5000 IU under clinician guidance
- **Protect the daylight window**: keep some runs outdoors at midday during October and early November while UVB is still marginal

## Key Takeaways: Vitamin D and Winter Running Performance

Vitamin D sets a floor for consistent winter training - not a ceiling to push through for extra speed.

- **Check status first**: a 25(OH)D test in October tells you whether you need correction or only maintenance
- **Correct a real deficit**: work with a clinician; expect health gains, not time improvements on the track
- **Immune stability and bone protection** are the real dividends, and they compound into training blocks that actually stick
- **Prioritise outdoor daylight runs in early autumn** to extend natural production before the UVB window closes
- **Do not over-supplement**: fat-soluble accumulation and the absence of performance benefits above sufficiency make topping up beyond 75-100 nmol/L unnecessary without medical guidance

A runner who stays healthy through November, December, and January will arrive at spring races with more consistent miles in the bank than one chasing a supplement-fuelled performance shortcut.
