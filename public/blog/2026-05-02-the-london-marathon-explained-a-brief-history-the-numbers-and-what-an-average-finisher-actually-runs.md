# The London Marathon Explained: A Brief History, the Numbers, and What an Average Finisher Actually Runs

Every April, tens of thousands of runners pour through the streets of London, past the Cutty Sark, over Tower Bridge, and down The Mall toward Buckingham Palace. This guide covers **The London Marathon Explained: A Brief History, the Numbers, and What an Average Finisher Actually Runs**, from its 1981 origins to the data behind today's massive field.

![London Marathon runners crossing Tower Bridge with the city skyline in the background](/blog/assets/the-london-marathon-explained-a-brief-history-the-numbers-and-what-an-average-finisher-actually-runs/image_1.jpg)

The race was founded by Olympic athletes Chris Brasher and John Disley, inspired by the New York City Marathon. The first edition in 1981 drew just over 6,255 finishers. Today, more than 56,000 people cross the finish line each year, drawn from a ballot that attracted over a million applicants for the 2026 edition alone.

## What Is the London Marathon?

The TCS London Marathon is one of seven Abbott World Marathon Majors, alongside Berlin, Boston, Chicago, New York, Tokyo, and Sydney. It covers 26.2 miles (42.195 km) each April, starting on Blackheath and in Greenwich Park, then finishing on The Mall, just yards from Buckingham Palace.

## A Brief History: From 1981 to Today

Chris Brasher ran the 1979 New York City Marathon and returned convinced London needed something similar. The inaugural race on 29 March 1981 drew more than 22,000 applicants; 6,255 crossed the finish line. Dick Beardsley and Inge Simonsen crossed together hand-in-hand in 2:11:48, and Joyce Smith won the women's race in 2:29:57.

Wheelchair racing joined the programme in 1982. David Weir won eight times and Tanni Grey-Thompson claimed six titles, making them the most decorated wheelchair champions in the event's history. The race moved to October for 2020 to 2022 because of COVID-19, then returned to April. The 2025 edition set a world record for any marathon, with 56,640 finishers.

## Key Records and Champions

Kelvin Kiptum set the men's course record of 2:01:25 in 2023. Eliud Kipchoge holds the record for most victories with four wins. Paula Radcliffe's 2:15:25 from 2003 remained a world record for 16 years. Tigst Assefa broke the women-only London record in 2025 with 2:15:50. Marcel Hug holds the men's wheelchair record of 1:23:44, and Catherine Debrunner set the women's wheelchair record of 1:34:18 in 2025.

## What the Average London Marathon Finisher Actually Runs

### Finish Time Distribution

Analysis of GPS-tracked runners reveals clear clustering just inside the three-hour, four-hour, and five-hour marks. More than 54% of GPS-tracked finishers completed the race in under four hours. For recreational runners, the realistic finish window sits between 3:30 and 5:00 or beyond, depending on training background, age, and race-day conditions.

### Pacing and Heart Rate Data

![Recreational runner checking GPS watch at mile 18 on the London Marathon course near Canary Wharf](/blog/assets/the-london-marathon-explained-a-brief-history-the-numbers-and-what-an-average-finisher-actually-runs/image_3.jpg)

Sub-three-hour finishers average around 167 bpm across the full race. Six-hour finishers average closer to 154 bpm, partly because walk breaks reduce sustained load. Garmin data suggests roughly 70 kcal per kilometre regardless of finish time, though Coros data diverges significantly, a useful reminder that wearable estimates carry meaningful uncertainty.

Note that the Terra dataset covered 571 GPS-tracked runners, with faster runners over-represented, so the 54% sub-four-hour figure likely skews quicker than the full 56,000-person field.

### Participant Demographics

Nearly half the field (49.71%) is aged 18 to 39. The gender gap has narrowed steadily over recent years. In 2023, the United States led all non-UK nations with 3,214 finishers. Around 50% of entrants run for a charity, typically with a fundraising target of £2,000 or more.

## The Course: Key Landmarks

The course is famously flat, with roughly 119 to 138 metres of total elevation gain, making it one of the faster major marathon routes in the world. Seven world records have been set on it.

- **Mile 6** - The Cutty Sark, a 150-year-old tea clipper
- **Mile 13** - Tower Bridge, the halfway point and the race's most photographed moment
- **Miles 14-18** - Canary Wharf and the Isle of Dogs loop
- **Mile 24** - Big Ben and the Houses of Parliament
- **Mile 26.2** - The Mall, finishing in front of Buckingham Palace

## How to Get a Place

Entry routes include:

- **Public ballot** - Opens for roughly one week after each race. With over a million applicants for 2026, the success rate is approximately 1 in 20.
- **Good for Age** - Around 6,000 places for runners who meet qualifying times at certified events.
- **Charity bib** - Roughly half of all finishers run for a charity, committing to a fundraising target of typically £2,000 or more.
- **Club and championship entries** - Reserved for runners meeting specific performance standards.
- **International tour operators** - Packages for overseas runners who cannot access the ballot directly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long is the London Marathon?**
26.2 miles, or 42.195 kilometres.

**When did the London Marathon start?**
The first edition was held on 29 March 1981.

**What is the average finish time?**
GPS data suggests more than half the tracked field finishes under four hours, though the full field average is likely closer to four and a half hours.

**How many people run the London Marathon?**
The 2025 edition set a world record with 56,640 finishers. Around 59,000 starters are expected in 2026.

**How much money has the London Marathon raised for charity?**
Over £1.4 billion since 1981, with a single-year record of £73.5 million in 2024.

**Is the London Marathon a World Marathon Major?**
