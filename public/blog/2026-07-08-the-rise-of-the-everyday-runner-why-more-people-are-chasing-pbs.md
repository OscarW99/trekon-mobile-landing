# The Rise of the Everyday Runner: Why More People Are Chasing PBs

Something remarkable is happening on pavements, park paths, and city streets around the world. **The rise of the everyday runner: why more people are chasing PBs** has become one of the defining cultural stories of our time, with ordinary people pinning race bibs to their walls and logging personal bests with quiet pride. Strava grew from 55 million users in 2020 to over 180 million by late 2025, and nearly 1.1 million people entered the lottery for a single marathon spot in London, almost double the previous record.

![Everyday runners crossing a race finish line together, celebrating personal bests](/blog/assets/the-rise-of-the-everyday-runner-why-more-people-are-chasing-pbs/image_1.jpg)

This is not simply a fitness trend. It is a cultural shift driven by community, identity, and a growing desire for tangible achievement in a screen-saturated age. Around 29% of current runners first laced up during the pandemic, finding in running a way to reclaim agency when everything else felt uncertain. What began as a coping mechanism has evolved into something far bigger, fuelled by inclusive run clubs and technology that makes chasing a PB feel genuinely within reach.

## What's Fuelling the Biggest Running Boom in History

The numbers alone tell a striking story. Forty per cent of the UK population now runs at least once a week, and 16 million people have laced up regularly. The London Marathon ballot smashed its previous record with 1.1 million applications for around 50,000 spots, making a bib statistically harder to secure than a place at some universities.

This is the third running wave, and it is unlike anything before it:

- **First wave**: competitive, club-driven, elite-focused
- **Second wave**: non-competitive participation events like colour runs and fun runs, broad but shallow in retention
- **Third wave**: community, identity, and a collective pivot back to performance, now available to everyone

The pendulum has swung decisively. Boston qualifying times tightened, the World Marathon Majors expanded, and more people from broader demographics are racing with genuine purpose.

## The Rise of the Everyday Runner: Why More People Are Chasing PBs Right Now

### From Lockdown Habit to Lasting Identity

Around 28.8% of current runners started during COVID-19, not because of a health campaign, but because running offered something screens could not: a body in motion, in real space, with a tangible sense of agency. Repetitive, bilateral motion is linked to reduced anxiety and improved emotional regulation, giving runners a neurological reason to keep going long after lockdowns ended. What began as a coping mechanism hardened into identity.

As [Gen Z logs off and laces up](https://lstvww.com/2026/06/02/everyone-is-running-right-now/), the trend has only accelerated, with Strava's 2025 report confirming that younger generations are actively choosing movement over passive scrolling.

### The Three Waves of Running Culture

Running's evolution moves through three distinct phases. The first was competitive and club-driven. The second prioritised participation without performance, which broadened the base but hurt retention. The third merges inclusivity with renewed competitive ambition. The current boom is not just bigger; it is stickier. Runners are staying, progressing, and chasing times rather than simply finishing.

## Who Is Running Today - and Why It's Different This Time

### Gen Z Is Rewriting the Rules

Runners aged 14-29 are the fastest-growing demographic in the sport. More than a third of UK entries to the 2026 London Marathon ballot came from 18-29-year-olds. Gen Z women, in particular, are driving participation surges across distances and platforms.

What is different about this cohort is their motivation:

- Run clubs are replacing nights out as the primary social venue
- Gear identity (Hoka, Lululemon, Salomon) is part of the appeal, not an afterthought
- [Gen Z is 75% more likely than Gen X](https://www.startribune.com/running-is-having-a-moment-and-young-people-are-fueling-the-boom/601597757) to cite a race or event as their primary exercise motivation

### Women Are Leading the Charge

![Women runners at a parkrun event, diverse ages and backgrounds, crossing the finish line together](/blog/assets/the-rise-of-the-everyday-runner-why-more-people-are-chasing-pbs/image_2.jpg)

Women represented 49.55% of UK applications to the 2026 London Marathon ballot, and 45% of the 56,640 finishers in the most recent edition were female. This is a remarkable shift given that women were banned from long road races until 1975. Parkrun has been central to this change: with 450,000 participants weekly, its ethos of participation over pace created a genuinely inclusive entry point, with women leading growth in the half marathon distance.

## The Psychology Behind Chasing a Personal Best

### Why PBs Matter More Than Ever in a Digital World

In an era of algorithmic reward and infinite scroll, a personal best is refreshingly concrete. You either ran it or you did not. The finish line does not negotiate.

This is partly why 20-somethings are turning to marathons to navigate quarter-life uncertainty. Strava's 2025 data backs this up: 86% of runners on structured training plans achieved a PB that year, and most runners identify as beginner (26%) or intermediate (34%), meaning the PB-chasing culture is not reserved for the fast.

### How Community Fuels Performance

Run clubs function as social infrastructure, providing mentorship, routine, accountability, and shared milestones that make training sustainable. The parkrun ethos, where no one is last and every finish is celebrated, has normalised the idea that performance and participation can coexist. Endurance events once considered extreme are now being completed by parents, shift workers, and people in their 50s and 60s.

---

## How Technology and Structured Training Are Accelerating the Trend

### From Casual Jogger to Goal-Driven Runner

GPS watches, coaching apps, and workout tracking have collapsed the gap between casual jogger and goal-driven runner. Personalised training plans that adapt to fitness level, schedule, and injury history have made structured preparation accessible to anyone with a smartphone. Consistency, progressive overload, and accountability, once the preserve of elite athletes, are now standard features of the everyday runner's toolkit.

### The Gear, the Cost, and the Commitment

Running has become a significant economic force. Entry fees, GPS watches, coaching subscriptions, and race travel represent real financial commitment, and that investment signals something important: people are serious. "Runfluencers" on social media have turned training logs and race recaps into content, creating social accountability that keeps runners consistent and publicly committed to their goals.

## Is the Running Boom Here to Stay?

### Signs This Is a Cultural Shift, Not a Fad

The evidence for permanence is strong. London, Chicago, and New York all broke attendance records in 2024. Trail running is growing rapidly, offering a pace-free culture that attracts runners who want experience over time.

![Packed start line at a major city marathon, thousands of everyday runners of all ages and backgrounds ready to race](/blog/assets/the-rise-of-the-everyday-runner-why-more-people-are-chasing-pbs/image_3.jpg)

Key indicators that this is structural rather than cyclical:

- Parkrun continues to grow year on year with no signs of plateau
- Run club proliferation is accelerating in cities and suburbs alike
- Strava's user base grew 327% between 2020 and 2025
- Gen Z prioritises fitness spending over dating and entertainment

### What This Means for the Future of Everyday Running

The demographic broadening of running is its most important long-term signal. Older athletes are discovering that endurance improves with accumulated experience. Trail and fell running are welcoming beginners without gatekeeping. Local clubs are functioning as genuine entry points rather than elite filters.

PB-chasing is no longer the preserve of the fast. It is a universal motivation that scales from a first 5K to a sub-three-hour marathon, available to anyone willing to show up consistently. Running has become something more than sport: it is identity, community, and one of the few places left where real-world effort produces a result that no algorithm can replicate.
