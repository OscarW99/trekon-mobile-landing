# How to Push Through the Wall — In a Race and in Training

**How to Push Through the Wall** is one of the most searched topics among distance runners. That sudden, crushing moment when your legs go heavy, your pace drops, and your mind starts bargaining with you to stop is something nearly every marathon runner faces. Research suggests around 70% of marathoners experience it, typically near the 18-to-20-mile mark when glycogen stores run critically low.

![Runner pushing through fatigue in the final miles of a marathon race](/blog/assets/how-to-push-through-the-wall-in-a-race-and-in-training/image_1.jpg)

The wall is not inevitable. It is largely the result of three controllable factors: pacing, fueling, and training preparation. Get those three things right, and you cross the finish line with something left in the tank.

## What Is "The Wall" and Why Does It Happen?

The wall is a genuine physiological event. Your muscles and liver store carbohydrate as glycogen, and that supply is finite. Most runners carry enough to fuel roughly two hours of steady aerobic effort. When glycogen runs critically low, your body shifts to burning fat, a far less efficient fuel source, causing a sudden dramatic slowdown around mile 18 to 20.

Your brain also plays a role. The central governor theory suggests your brain begins throttling output before you are truly empty, as a protective mechanism. That is why the wall often feels like a mental shutdown as much as a physical one. Dehydration compounds everything, accelerating fatigue and making glycogen depletion hit harder and faster.

## Warning Signs You're About to Hit the Wall

The wall rarely arrives without warning. Catching the early signals gives you time to respond.

- **Pace drop with high effort:** Your GPS shows a slower split, but your effort feels the same or harder.
- **Heavy legs and foggy head:** Both signal glycogen running low.
- **Form breakdown:** Shoulders creeping up, stride shortening, and loss of coordination.
- **Negative self-talk:** Thoughts like "I just want to stop" are neurological warning signals, not facts.

If you notice two or more of these together, act immediately on fueling and pace.

## Training Strategies to Prevent the Wall

![Runner doing a progression long run on a trail, finishing strong in the final miles](/blog/assets/how-to-push-through-the-wall-in-a-race-and-in-training/image_2.jpg)

The most effective wall prevention happens in training, weeks before race day. Three areas matter most.

### Build a Strong Aerobic Base

A wide aerobic base is your best insurance policy. Easy mileage builds the foundation that lets you handle race-specific stress without breaking down.

- Allow 16 to 20 weeks for a full marathon training block.
- Stack consistent weeks rather than chasing big single-week mileage spikes.
- Prioritise injury resistance: consistent training over 16 weeks beats hard training over 10 that ends in injury.

### Use Progression Long Runs

Running the second half of your long run faster than the first simulates late-race conditions on tired legs.

- Run the first half easy, then build to marathon pace for the last 4 to 5 miles on your longest runs.
- Limit marathon-pace long runs to no more than six per training cycle to avoid overtraining.

As [McMillan Running's finish-strong framework](https://www.mcmillanrunning.com/more-power-finish-strong-marathon/) explains, the goal is to reset your central governor by consistently exposing it to higher effort levels in training.

### Practice Fueling in Every Key Workout

Your gut is trainable. Practicing fueling in training means no surprises on race day.

- Take gels on every long run of 16 miles or more.
- Use a 30 to 45 minute fueling cadence to match your planned race-day strategy.
- Never introduce a new gel, drink, or food on race day.

## Race-Day Pacing: The #1 Wall Prevention Tool

Going out too fast is the most common reason runners hit the wall.

- For the first 5 miles, run approximately 10 seconds per mile slower than your goal pace.
- Use perceived effort rather than GPS pace as your primary guide in the first third of the race.
- Monitor heart rate drift: if your heart rate climbs while pace holds steady, back off slightly.

The goal is to reach mile 20 feeling like you have something left, not already in survival mode.

## Race-Day Fueling and Hydration

Fueling and pacing are inseparable. Even perfect pacing cannot fully compensate for skipped gels.

- Start carb-loading 3 days before race day to maximise glycogen stores.
- Take a gel approximately 15 minutes before the race starts.
- Aim for 60 to 90 grams of carbohydrate per hour using gels, sports drinks, or energy chews.
- Fuel every 30 to 45 minutes throughout the race, not just when you feel tired.
- Sip fluids consistently at aid stations and include electrolytes, particularly sodium.

## What to Do If You Hit the Wall Mid-Race

Even with perfect preparation, the wall can still find you. Acting quickly gives you the best chance of salvaging your race.

- Take an extra gel immediately, then fuel every 20 minutes for the remainder of the race.
- Slow down by 15 to 30 seconds per mile to bring effort back into a sustainable range.
- Relax your shoulders, check your form, and reset your stride.
- Focus only on the next mile marker, not the total distance remaining.
- Replace negative thoughts with controllable phrases: "I am in control" and "I can keep moving forward."

## Mental Strategies That Work

The mental side of the wall is as real as the physical side, and it responds to training just as well.

- **Visualisation:** Before race day, mentally rehearse miles 18 to 22. Anticipate the discomfort and picture yourself moving through it with control.
- **Segment focus:** Break the race into chunks of 2 to 3 miles. You are never running 26.2 miles; you are running to the next landmark.
- **Positive self-talk:** Affirmations like "Strong to the line" interrupt the brain's shutdown signal.
- **Target a fellow runner:** In the final miles, pick a runner ahead and gradually close the gap for a concrete, achievable goal.

Mental barriers are signals to respond to, not verdicts to accept. Build mental toughness the same way you build fitness: gradually and consistently. Simulate race conditions by doing strides at the end of long runs, and add one mental tool per week, starting with chunking, then body-focused cues, then positive self-talk.

## Putting It All Together: Your Wall-Proof Race Plan

![Runner taking an energy gel at a race aid station during a marathon](/blog/assets/how-to-push-through-the-wall-in-a-race-and-in-training/image_3.jpg)

Preparation across all three areas: training, pacing, and fueling, is what separates runners who fade from runners who finish strong.

| Phase | Action |
| --- | --- |
| Race week | Carb-load for 3 days; finalise gel and hydration plan |
| Race morning | Pre-race gel 15 minutes before start |
| Miles 1-5 | Run 10 seconds per mile slower than goal pace |
| Miles 6-20 | Settle into goal pace; fuel every 30-45 minutes |
| Miles 20-26 | Use mental cues; focus on next mile only |
| Post-race | Analyse pacing and fueling data; adjust for next time |

The wall is not a sign that you are not fit enough. It is almost always a sign that pacing, fueling, or training preparation had a gap. Close those gaps systematically and the wall becomes something you read about rather than something you experience.
