# The Mona Fartlek: Twenty Minutes That Will Humble You

**The Mona Fartlek: Twenty Minutes That Will Humble You** is not an exaggeration. Named after Australian four-time Olympian Steve Moneghetti and devised by his coach Chris Wardlaw, this deceptively simple 20-minute session has been making runners faster and more honest about their fitness since the 1980s.

No track required, no fancy gear needed - just a watch and the willingness to keep moving.

![Runner pushing through a hard fartlek interval on an open road, mid-stride with focused expression](/blog/assets/the-mona-fartlek-twenty-minutes-that-will-humble-you/image_1.jpg)

The workout descends through 14 hard efforts paired with equal-duration "floats": 2 x 90 seconds, 4 x 60 seconds, 4 x 30 seconds, and 4 x 15 seconds. Hard means roughly your 5K pace; floats are controlled, continuous running - not a slow jog. You never fully stop, and that sustained effort is precisely where the session earns its reputation.

What makes it so effective is the self-regulating structure. As the intervals shorten, fatigue accumulates, and the floats reveal exactly where your fitness truly stands.

## What Is the Mona Fartlek?

Steve Moneghetti is an Australian marathon legend who represented his country at four consecutive Olympic Games, from Seoul 1988 to Sydney 2000. A 10,000m specialist turned world-class marathoner, he ran a 2:08:16 marathon PB and a world record 1:00:06 half marathon.

The session was devised by coach Chris Wardlaw, not by Moneghetti himself. Wardlaw built it on fartlek principles - "fartlek" is the Swedish for "speed play," created in the 1930s to blend speed and endurance without full rest breaks.

The Mona's core is 20 minutes of continuous running. You never stop, and the floats between hard efforts keep you moving the entire time.

## The Mona Fartlek: Twenty Minutes That Will Humble You - The Full Workout Structure

![Steve Moneghetti racing in his Australian kit at the 1992 Barcelona Olympics, mid-stride on the marathon course](/blog/assets/the-mona-fartlek-twenty-minutes-that-will-humble-you/image_2.jpg)

The session follows a descending ladder with 14 hard efforts, each matched by an equal-duration float. The 20-minute core breaks down like this:

| Block | Hard Effort | Float Recovery |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Block 1 | 2 × 90 seconds | 90 seconds |
| Block 2 | 4 × 60 seconds | 60 seconds |
| Block 3 | 4 × 30 seconds | 30 seconds |
| Block 4 | 4 × 15 seconds | 15 seconds |

Bracket the 20-minute core with proper easy running:
- Warm-up: 10-15 minutes easy to raise heart rate and prime the legs
- Main set: 20 minutes, all 14 pairs, no stopping at any point
- Cooldown: 10 minutes easy to flush metabolic fatigue

## What "Hard" and "Float" Actually Mean

"Hard" scales with interval length. The 90-second reps sit around 10K race pace, the 60-second reps at 5K pace, the 30s at 3K effort, and the 15s close to mile pace or near-maximal.

The float is not a slow jog. Think of it as marathon pace - controlled, purposeful, continuous - enough to keep heart rate elevated in the aerobic zone while allowing partial recovery for the next effort.

Key effort targets by block:
- 2 × 90 seconds: approximately 10K race pace
- 4 × 60 seconds: 5K race pace
- 4 × 30 seconds: 3K or 2-mile race pace
- 4 × 15 seconds: mile effort or near-maximal

## Why the Float Is the Secret Weapon

The float is what separates the Mona from ordinary interval training. By never fully stopping, you keep cardiovascular stress elevated for the full 20 minutes, accumulating aerobic load across all 14 efforts.

Walking or slow jogging between reps drops heart rate too far and turns the session into disconnected sprints. The float demands real discipline, especially when the temptation to crawl is strongest in the 30s and 15s blocks.

Three keys to float discipline:
- Start conservatively in the early 90-second floats to protect the back half of the session
- Aim for consistent float pace across all 14 intervals, not faster early and slower late
- As fitness improves over weeks, speed up floats while keeping hard efforts the same

## The Benefits of Doing the Mona Fartlek

### How The Mona Fartlek: Twenty Minutes That Will Humble You Builds Speed Endurance

The descending structure pushes both aerobic and anaerobic systems in a single 20-minute block. Longer reps build lactate threshold tolerance; shorter reps target fast-twitch recruitment and sharpen top-end speed.

Runners consistently see gains in 5K and 10K performance. The session teaches the body to sustain faster paces as fatigue accumulates, which is exactly the skill race day tests.

### Why It Works for Runners of All Levels

The Mona is time-based, not distance-based, so faster runners simply cover more ground in the same structure. A club runner and an elite share the identical 20-minute session but work at different absolute paces.

Distance benchmarks for the 20-minute core:
- Elite men: 6 km or more; elite women: around 5.5 km
- Serious club men: around 5.5 km; serious women: around 5 km
- Club runners: approximately 5 km (men) and 4.5 km (women)

## How to Pace Yourself Through All 14 Intervals

### The Mona Fartlek: Twenty Minutes That Will Humble You - Common Pacing Mistakes

Most runners go too hard in the 90-second reps and fade badly through the 30s and 15s blocks. The longer efforts should feel strong and controlled, not maximum - save the sharpest acceleration for the shortest reps.

Avoid these three errors:
- Starting too fast: the opening 90s reps feel manageable, setting up a painful second-half fade
- Floating too slowly: shuffling between efforts removes the continuous aerobic stimulus that makes the session work
- Form collapse under fatigue: hunching and over-striding waste energy; focus on arm drive, tall posture, and quick cadence

## Where to Fit the Mona Fartlek in Your Training Week

Run the Mona on a designated speed day with at least one easy day either side. It suits marathon build phases and 10K training blocks equally well, delivering genuine speed work without heavy track volume.

Use it strategically:
- Once per week as your primary speed session during a build phase
- Every 4-6 weeks as a benchmark to track fitness changes objectively
- Early in a training cycle to establish a speed baseline before longer workouts

## How to Track Progress With the Mona Fartlek

The simplest metric is total distance covered in 20 minutes. Combine this with average pace and average heart rate to get a three-number fitness snapshot you can compare across sessions.

Return every 4-6 weeks and look for:
- Increased total distance at the same perceived effort
- Faster float pace in the final 4 × 15-second block
- Lower average heart rate for the same hard-effort pace, signalling improved aerobic fitness

## Tips to Get the Most Out of Every Session

Choose a flat, uninterrupted route with minimal traffic lights and good footing. Flat terrain allows consistent pacing, and interruptions break the continuous stimulus that drives the session's training effect.

Practical checklist for every session:
- Use a GPS watch with an interval timer - manual checking disrupts concentration and pacing
- Cue arm drive and cadence when fatigue hits, on floats as much as on hard reps
- Progress the floats over multiple weeks, not single sessions - let the adaptation catch up before pushing harder
