# Stack Height, Drop, and Heel-Toe Offset: A Plain-English Shoe Guide

If you've ever stared at a running shoe spec sheet and felt more confused than when you started, you're not alone. Stack height, drop, and heel-toe offset all matter, but nobody seems to explain them in plain language.

![Diagram showing stack height and heel-to-toe drop measurements on a running shoe cross-section](/blog/assets/stack-height-drop-and-heel-toe-offset-a-plain-english-shoe-guide/image_1.jpg)

Here's the short version: stack height is the total foam between your foot and the ground, while heel-to-toe drop is the height difference between the heel and the forefoot. A shoe with a 37 mm heel stack and a 27 mm forefoot stack has a 10 mm drop. These two numbers are related but completely independent - you can have a highly cushioned shoe with zero drop, or a minimal shoe with a steep heel angle.

Understanding both measurements helps you make smarter choices about comfort, biomechanics, and injury prevention.

## What These Numbers Actually Mean (And Why Runners Get Them Wrong)

The confusion starts because drop and stack height sound like they describe the same thing. They don't. Stack height tells you how much material sits between your foot and the ground. Drop tells you the angle created by the difference between heel and forefoot stack. You can have a massively cushioned shoe with zero drop, or a thin shoe with a steep heel angle. Neither number predicts the other.

The real-world consequence of mixing these up is buying the wrong shoe for the wrong reason. A runner avoiding "high-stack" shoes because they think it means a steep heel angle might miss out on cushioning they actually need.

## Core Definitions: Stack Height and Drop Explained

### What Is Stack Height?

Stack height is the total thickness of midsole foam between the bottom of your foot and the ground, measured in millimeters.

- **High stack (35 mm+):** Maximum cushioning, less ground feel. Examples include the Hoka Bondi and New Balance Fresh Foam More.
- **Low stack (under 20 mm):** More ground feel and proprioception, less shock absorption. Examples include the Merrell Vapor Glove and New Balance Minimus.

### What Is Heel-Toe Drop (Offset)?

Drop is the vertical difference between heel stack and forefoot stack, measured in millimeters.

**Formula:** Heel stack (mm) - Forefoot stack (mm) = Drop (mm)

The common range runs from 0 mm to about 15 mm. This measurement goes by several names: drop, offset, heel-toe drop, pitch, and heel differential. All describe the same thing: how much higher your heel sits compared to your forefoot.

### Why Drop and Stack Height Are Not the Same Thing

Think of two runners on different surfaces. One stands on a thick foam mat that is perfectly flat - high stack, zero drop. The other stands on a thin wedge tilting their heel upward - low stack, high drop. The amount of foam and the angle of the foot are completely independent variables.

A practical example: the Saucony Kinvara has a 4 mm drop with a modest stack, giving a light feel. The New Balance Fresh Foam More also sits around 4 mm drop but with a dramatically higher stack. Same drop, very different ride.

## How to Read a Shoe Spec Sheet

### Drop Categories in Plain English

Every drop number comes from one calculation: **Heel stack - Forefoot stack = Drop**

- **Zero drop (0 mm):** Heel and forefoot sit at the same height. Common in Altra and Vivobarefoot.
- **Low drop (1-4 mm):** Slight angle, close to natural foot position. Places higher demand on calves and the Achilles tendon.
- **Mid drop (5-8 mm):** Works well for a wide range of runners and is a good starting point when switching from traditional shoes.
- **High drop (8-12+ mm):** The traditional trainer range. Reduces Achilles load and suits heel-strike runners. The most common single value is around 10 mm.

For a deeper look at how brands report these numbers, [Running Warehouse's heel-toe drop guide](https://www.runningwarehouse.com/learningcenter/gear_guides/footwear/heel-toe-drop.html) is a reliable reference.

## Biomechanics: How These Numbers Affect Your Running

### How Drop Affects Foot Strike

- **High drop shoes** tend to accommodate heel striking and shift load toward the knees and hips.
- **Low drop shoes** encourage midfoot or forefoot striking, increasing ankle flexion and demand on the calves and Achilles.

Neither landing pattern is universally superior. The right drop matches your current biomechanics and injury history.

### The Four Combinations You'll Actually Encounter

| Combination | Feel | Best Use Case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High stack + high drop | Plush, elevated heel | Long road runs, heel strikers, recovery days |
| High stack + low drop | Cushioned but flat | Protection without heel elevation |
| Low stack + high drop | Firm, angled | Lightweight trainers, racing flats |
| Low stack + low drop | Minimal, ground-close | Barefoot-style training, short efforts |

## How to Choose the Right Drop and Stack

### Matching Drop to Your Injury History

- **Achilles tightness or calf soreness:** Start with 8-12 mm drop to reduce tension on the posterior chain.
- **Knee pain or hip discomfort:** Consider 0-6 mm drop to reduce heel-strike loading.
- **Flat feet:** A moderate drop (6-8 mm) often provides comfortable support.
- **High arches:** Lower drop allows more natural foot motion and can improve stability.

### Transitioning to a New Drop Safely

Changing drop is one of the most common causes of running injuries. The rule of thumb is a two-week minimum transition window.

- Stick to easy, flat runs during the first two weeks.
- Avoid hills and speed work until your body has adapted.
- Watch for morning calf or Achilles stiffness as an early warning sign.

[Trekon](https://www.trekon.run) offers structured training plans that help you build mileage progressively while adapting to a new shoe setup.

### Should You Rotate Shoes With Different Drops?

Rotating between two pairs with slightly different drops is an underrated injury-prevention strategy. Varied sensory input challenges different movement patterns and prevents repetitive stress. A practical rotation might pair a 10 mm drop daily trainer with a 4-6 mm drop lighter shoe for shorter efforts.

| Metric | What It Measures | Choose Based On |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stack height | Total foam between foot and ground | Cushioning needs, surface type, body weight |
| Heel-toe drop | Height difference between heel and forefoot | Landing mechanics, injury history, muscle engagement |

- **Choose stack height** based on how much cushioning you need for your typical surface and mileage.
- **Choose drop** based on where you land, where you feel soreness, and how much Achilles or knee load you want to manage.
- **The golden rule:** always evaluate both numbers together. A 10 mm drop in a low-stack shoe feels completely different from a 10 mm drop in a 40 mm stack shoe. Neither number alone tells the full story.
