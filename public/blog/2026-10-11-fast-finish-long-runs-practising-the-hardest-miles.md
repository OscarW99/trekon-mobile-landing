# Fast-Finish Long Runs: Practising the Hardest Miles

Every marathoner knows the feeling: legs heavy, glycogen fading, and miles still left to race. **Fast-Finish Long Runs: Practising the Hardest Miles** is the training strategy built specifically for that moment, conditioning your body and mind to sustain pace when fatigue sets in hardest.

![Runner accelerating through the final miles of a long training run on an open road](/blog/assets/fast-finish-long-runs-practising-the-hardest-miles/image_1.jpg)

The concept is straightforward but demanding. You begin at easy, comfortable pace, then shift into marathon pace for the final 20-40% of the distance, typically the last 5-8 kilometres.

That late surge forces your legs to recruit fast-twitch muscle fibres on depleted fuel, teaching your body to burn fat more efficiently. Repeated practice sharpens mental resilience, improves running economy under stress, and builds genuine race-day confidence.

## What Is a Fast-Finish Long Run?

A fast-finish long run starts easy and transitions into a structured faster effort for the final 20-40% of the distance, roughly 5-15 km (3-9 miles). The faster block targets marathon pace or goal effort, not an all-out sprint.

- Easy opening miles covering 60-80% of the total distance
- Faster finish block at marathon or tempo effort
- Brief easy cooldown to close the session

Most runners aim for 5-8 km of faster work. The acceleration is controlled and purposeful, not improvised.

## Why the Final Miles Are the Hardest Part of Marathon Training

Glycogen stores are depleted by miles 18-20, forcing the body to recruit fast-twitch muscle fibres not built for sustained endurance. Pace fades and form breaks down. Fast-finish long runs rehearse this scenario so it feels familiar rather than catastrophic on race day.

## The Science Behind Fast-Finish Long Runs: Practising the Hardest Miles

![Runner checking pace on a GPS watch during a structured long run workout](/blog/assets/fast-finish-long-runs-practising-the-hardest-miles/image_2.jpg)

Training on depleted glycogen teaches the body to oxidise fat more efficiently, a critical skill for holding marathon pace in the closing miles. Repeating race-specific effort under fatigue builds muscle durability and improves running economy under stress.

Key physiological adaptations:
- Improved fat metabolism and glycogen conservation late in effort
- Greater fatigue resistance and leg durability across the full distance
- Better stride efficiency and form retention under accumulated load

These gains compound across a training block rather than emerging from a single session.

## Who Should (and Shouldn't) Do Fast-Finish Long Runs

This workout suits runners already comfortable completing 14-16 mile long runs with good form. Beginners should build consistent easy distance before adding any structured pace work.

Finishing hard on fatigued muscles can degrade form and raise tissue stress. A safer alternative is a faster middle segment rather than a hard close at the end.

Runners who should wait:
- Those completing their first marathon training cycle
- Runners managing injury or chronic niggles
- Anyone whose current long run already causes significant form breakdown

## How to Structure a Fast-Finish Long Run: Practising the Hardest Miles

Begin at an easy, conversational pace for the first 60-75% of planned distance, then ramp gradually into the faster block. Take a gel 15-20 minutes before the transition and maintain consistent hydration. Finish with at least one easy kilometre to promote recovery.

### Step-by-Step Example Workout

For an 18-mile session:
- Miles 1-13 at easy, conversational pace
- Miles 13-16 at 10-15 seconds faster than goal marathon pace
- Mile 17 at hard, race-day effort
- Mile 18 as easy cooldown

If fatigue feels excessive before the transition, keep the entire run easy - a healthy easy long run always beats a compromised fast-finish attempt.

### Progression Plan Across a Training Block

Aim for four fast-finish long runs every other week, starting 8-10 weeks before race day.

| Week | Total Distance | Fast Finish Segment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Week 8 | 14-16 miles | Last 4 miles at goal pace |
| Week 10 | 15-17 miles | Last 5-6 miles at goal pace |
| Week 12 | 18-20 miles | Last 8-10 miles at goal pace |

More than four sessions risks peaking too early. Taper normally after week 12-13.

## How Often Should You Do Fast-Finish Long Runs?

Alternate fast-finish long runs with standard easy long runs every other week, never scheduling them back-to-back. Target 2-3 sessions in the final 8 weeks before your marathon.

- Alternate with easy long runs so each block has adequate recovery
- Reduce other speedwork during weeks that include a fast-finish session
- Revert to an easy long run if cumulative fatigue is running high

Recovery between sessions is where adaptation happens.

## Pacing, Fuelling, and Form: Getting the Details Right

Use a GPS watch to enter the faster block at the right effort, then shift to feel-based running rather than chasing splits. Monitor cadence, breathing, and posture as reliable effort signals.

Practise your exact race-day gel timing so nothing is unfamiliar come race morning. For a deeper look, [Speedwork for marathoners: the fast-finish long run](https://runningmagazine.ca/sections/training/speedwork-for-marathoners-the-fast-finish-long-run/) is a useful reference.

## The Mental Side: Training Your Mind for the Toughest Miles

Every fast-finish long run is a mental rep. You rehearse the moment fatigue says "I'm done" and train yourself to find one more gear. Each time you win that fight in training, race-day confidence grows.

Running the faster block without fixating on splits builds genuine body awareness, replacing technology as a crutch with honest feel for effort and form.

## Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

![Tired runner maintaining form and posture in the final miles of a long training run](/blog/assets/fast-finish-long-runs-practising-the-hardest-miles/image_3.jpg)

Avoid these errors to extract maximum benefit from every fast-finish session:

- **Starting too fast**: drifting into marathon pace before the designated transition defeats the purpose and raises injury risk
- **Skipping the cooldown**: the final easy kilometre is essential recovery, not optional
- **Running it every week**: without alternating easy long runs, fatigue accumulates faster than fitness
- **Ignoring fatigue signals**: if form is already breaking down, drop the faster block and keep the run easy
- **Arriving under-fuelled**: no gel plan means glycogen runs out before the faster segment begins

## Frequently Asked Questions About Fast-Finish Long Runs

### What pace should the fast finish be?

Target marathon pace for the bulk of the faster block. Advanced runners can push 10-15 seconds per kilometre faster in the final 2-3 km, approaching a tempo effort.

### Can beginners try fast-finish long runs?

Build to a comfortable 14-16 mile long run first. Adding pace on an underdeveloped aerobic base introduces stress the body is not yet ready to absorb safely.

### Is a fast finish the same as a negative split?

A negative split is a race strategy: the second half of the race is faster than the first. A fast-finish long run is a training workout with a defined faster block placed deliberately at the end. Both involve finishing fast, but the goals differ. [Fast Finish Long Runs: How and Why To Do Them](https://therunningchannel.com/fast-finish-long-run/) covers the distinction in detail.

### How do fast-finish long runs differ from tempo runs?

A tempo run is a sustained threshold effort on fresh legs. A fast-finish long run layers race-specific speed on top of accumulated fatigue, making it uniquely specific to marathon preparation rather than general speed development.
